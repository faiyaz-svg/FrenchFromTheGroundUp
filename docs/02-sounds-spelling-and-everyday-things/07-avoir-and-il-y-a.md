# Avoir and il y a

After this lesson you can use all six forms of *avoir* ("to have"), say your age, say that you are hungry, thirsty, hot, cold or sleepy, and say that something exists with *il y a*.

Before this: [Numbers from 0 to 100](06-numbers-0-100.md), [Plurals, des and les](05-plurals-and-des-les.md), [Être and introducing yourself](../01-first-sounds-and-greetings/10-etre-and-introductions.md). You need: nothing.

## The idea

*Avoir* /avwaʁ/ is one of the most used verbs in French, with *être*. It means "to have", like "I have a book". It also helps to say your age, how you feel and what is in a place, and in these cases English uses "to be". This lesson shows you each of those uses. Remember the term **conjugate** from the *être* lesson: to give the forms of a verb for each subject.

### The present of avoir

| Pronoun | Form | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| *je* | *ai* | /ʒe/ (written *j'ai*) |
| *tu* | *as* | /ty a/ |
| *il / elle* | *a* | /il a/, /ɛl a/ |
| *nous* | *avons* | /nuz avɔ̃/ |
| *vous* | *avez* | /vuz ave/ |
| *ils / elles* | *ont* | /ilz ɔ̃/, /ɛlz ɔ̃/ |

Three things to listen for. First, *je ai* turns into *j'ai* by elision, and it sounds like the letter *é*, /e/. Second, *tu as* and *il a* have a silent final letter, so you only hear the vowel /a/. Third, the forms *nous avons*, *vous avez* and *ils ont* have the required liaison from the liaison lesson: /z/ joins the pronoun to the verb. These are the same liaison patterns you heard as a listening chunk. Also note that *il a* (he has) and *à* (to, at) sound alike, and only the spelling tells them apart.

### Having things and having an age

For things, *avoir* works as "to have": *J'ai un chat*, *tu as une clé*, *nous avons des livres*. For age, French uses *avoir* and the word *an* /ɑ̃/ (year): *J'ai vingt ans* means "I am twenty years old". You cannot say *je suis vingt ans*, and you cannot leave out *ans*. After a number above one, *an* takes an *s*: *un an*, *deux ans*.

```fr
Employée: Bonjour, monsieur. Vous avez une carte d'identité? | Hello, sir. Do you have an ID card?
Vous: Oui, voilà. | Yes, here you are.
Employée: Merci. Vous avez trente ans? | Thank you. Are you thirty?
Vous: Non, j'ai quarante-deux ans. | No, I'm forty-two.
Employée: Parfait, merci. Bonne journée. | Perfect, thank you. Have a good day.
```

### Feelings with avoir

French says "I have hunger" where English says "I am hungry". These are **idioms**: fixed phrases whose meaning you learn whole.

| French | Meaning | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| *avoir faim* | to be hungry | /fɛ̃/ |
| *avoir soif* | to be thirsty | /swaf/ |
| *avoir chaud* | to be hot (warm) | /ʃo/ |
| *avoir froid* | to be cold | /fʁwa/ |
| *avoir sommeil* | to be sleepy | /sɔmɛj/ |
| *avoir raison* | to be right | /ʁɛzɔ̃/ |
| *avoir tort* | to be wrong | /tɔʁ/ |

You say *J'ai faim*, *tu as soif*, *nous avons froid*. You do not use *être* here, and *Je suis chaud* means something different, so do not say it.

Two more phrases use a noun after them: *avoir besoin de* means "to need" and *avoir envie de* means "to feel like having" or "to want". With a noun, *de* comes before the article: *J'ai besoin d'un café* (I need a coffee), *j'ai envie d'une pomme* (I feel like an apple). The *de* shortens to *d'* before a vowel, and *un* and *une* stay the same. Words like *du* come later, so keep to *un* and *une* for now.

### Il y a: there is, there are

*Il y a* /il i a/ means both "there is" and "there are". It never changes, whatever follows. It is the word for saying that something exists or is in a place: *Il y a un café ici* (there is a café here), *Il y a des livres sur la table* (there are some books on the table). It has the form *a* of *avoir* inside it, and it is a fixed phrase, so you learn it as one unit.

The pieces are *il*, *y* and *a*. The *y* here means "there", and it is a short word that you will see again. For now, treat *il y a* as a chunk. When you want to ask, raise your voice at the end: *Il y a un café ici?* A negative form, *il n'y a pas de*, will come in [Saying no: negation](../03-verbs-and-questions/03-negation.md), and you only need to recognise it now.

Do not mix up *il y a* with *c'est*. Use *c'est* to name something you point at (*C'est un livre*) and *il y a* to say what is in a place (*Il y a un livre ici*).

### Avoir or être?

A short test helps you choose. Age, hunger, thirst, temperature and sleep use *avoir*. Names, nationalities, jobs and places use *être*. *J'ai vingt ans, je suis canadien*. A common slip is *je suis faim*, which an English speaker says by copying "I am hungry".

```fr
Sam: J'ai faim. Et toi? | I'm hungry. And you?
Nadia: Moi aussi, j'ai très faim. Il y a des œufs? | Me too, I'm very hungry. Are there any eggs?
Sam: Oui, il y a des œufs et un pain. | Yes, there are eggs and a loaf of bread.
Nadia: Parfait. Tu as soif? Moi, j'ai envie d'un thé. | Perfect. Are you thirsty? I feel like having a tea.
Sam: Oui, j'ai soif. Merci! | Yes, I'm thirsty. Thanks!
```

## Hear it

Listen for the /z/ in *nous avons*, *vous avez* and *ils ont*, and for the silent final letters of *tu as* and *il a*.

```fr
J'ai, tu as, il a. | I have, you have, he has.
Nous avons, vous avez, ils ont. | We have, you have, they have.
J'ai vingt ans. Tu as dix-huit ans. | I'm twenty. You're eighteen.
J'ai faim, j'ai soif, j'ai sommeil. | I'm hungry, I'm thirsty, I'm sleepy.
Il a raison, elle a tort. | He's right, she's wrong.
Il y a un problème. Il y a des chats. | There is a problem. There are some cats.
```

## Try it

1. Write the right form of *avoir*: *Je ___ faim. Nous ___ un chat. Elles ___ vingt ans. Tu ___ raison.*
2. Say in French: "I am cold." "She is thirty." "We are hungry."
3. Say in French: "There is a café here." "There are three books."
4. *Avoir* or *être*? ___ chaud. ___ canadien. ___ vingt ans.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. *J'ai faim. Nous avons un chat. Elles ont vingt ans. Tu as raison.*
2. *J'ai froid. Elle a trente ans. Nous avons faim.*
3. *Il y a un café ici. Il y a trois livres.*
4. *J'ai chaud* (*avoir*). *Je suis canadien* (*être*). *J'ai vingt ans* (*avoir*).

</details>

## Say it

```fr
J'ai vingt ans. | I'm twenty years old.
Tu as faim? Moi, j'ai soif. | Are you hungry? I'm thirsty.
Nous avons un ami à Montréal. | We have a friend in Montreal.
Il y a un café ici. | There is a café here.
Ils ont raison. | They are right.
```

## Common mistakes

Saying *je suis vingt ans* or *je suis faim*. Age and feelings use *avoir*: *j'ai vingt ans*, *j'ai faim*.

Leaving out *ans* when you give your age. English can say "I'm twenty", but French says *j'ai vingt ans*.

Writing *il a* as *il à* or the other way around. They sound the same, so check what the sentence needs: a verb (*il a*) or a small word meaning "to" (*à*).

## In Quebec

In everyday speech, *il y a* is often shortened to *y a*, which you will hear in Montreal and in France. Casual questions in Quebec<!-- link: ../03-verbs-and-questions/10-quebec-casual-questions.md --> explains the casual forms. Say *il y a* when you speak and write.
