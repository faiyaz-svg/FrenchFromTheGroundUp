# Spelling rules and liaison

<span class="tag tag-sounds">Sounds</span> <span class="tag tag-a1">A1</span>

After this lesson you can tell which letters at the end of a French word are silent, drop a vowel before another vowel (elision), and make or skip the link sound between words (liaison) in the most common cases.

Before this: [French consonants](01-consonants.md), [The French r and the semi-vowels](02-r-and-semi-vowels.md), [The alphabet and spelling your name](../01-first-sounds-and-greetings/07-alphabet-and-spelling-names.md). You need: nothing.

## The idea

French spelling keeps letters that the mouth no longer says. The word *petit* /pəti/ ends in a *t* you never hear, and *les amis* /lez ami/ has an *s* that you hear only because a vowel follows. This lesson gives four rules that explain most of these cases: silent letters, the *e* that disappears, the vowel that drops out (elision), and the link between words (liaison).

### Silent final letters and the silent e

You learned in the last lesson that most final consonants are silent, except the CaReFuL letters. A final *e* is silent too, and it makes the consonant before it pronounced. Compare *petit* /pəti/ (small, masculine) with *petite* /pətit/ (small, feminine): the only change is that the *t* now sounds. The same happens in *grand* /gʁɑ̃/ and *grande* /gʁɑ̃d/.

Inside a phrase, the *e* of small words such as *le*, *de* and *je* is often dropped as well. *Je suis* sounds like /ʒsɥi/ in fast speech. You will practise this rhythm in a later lesson.

### Elision

When a short word ending in a vowel meets a word that begins with a vowel, the vowel drops out and an apostrophe takes its place. This is **elision**. It happens with *le* and *la* (*l'hôtel*, *l'école*), *je* (*j'ai*, "I have"), *de* (*d'accord*, "okay"), *ne* (later), *que* (*qu'est-ce que*), *se*, *me*, *te* and *ce*. You have already used *m'appelle* and *c'est*, and *s'il vous plaît* has elision too. It never happens before a consonant: *le café*, not *l'café*.

Elision also happens before a silent *h*, which is where the next section starts.

### Two kinds of h

The letter *h* is never pronounced, but it behaves in two ways. In most words the **h muet** ("mute h") acts as if it were not there: elision and liaison happen. You say *l'homme* /lɔm/ (the man), *l'hôtel* and *l'heure*, and *les hommes* /lez ɔm/ links like a vowel.

In a smaller group of words, the **h aspiré** ("aspirated h") blocks both: there is no elision and no liaison. You say *le héros* /lə eʁo/ (the hero), *les héros* /le eʁo/ with a small break and no /z/, and *le hamburger*. The sound is not breath, it is a silent barrier. You cannot tell from the spelling alone which kind a word has, so learn it with the word. Dictionaries mark the h aspiré in their entries.

### Enchaînement and liaison

Two things happen when a word ending in a pronounced consonant meets a word starting with a vowel. In **enchaînement** ("linking"), the consonant slides over to the next word: *il est* /i lɛ/, *elle a* /ɛ la/. Nothing was silent, and the consonant only changes word. The result is that French sounds like a string of open syllables.

In **liaison** (from the word for "bond"), a silent final consonant comes back before a vowel. The rest of the time you do not hear it. *Les amis* /lez ami/ (the friends) has a /z/ that is not there in *les chats* or *les garçons*. That /z/ is the liaison consonant.

Four changes show up:

| Written | Sounds as | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *s*, *x*, *z* | /z/ | *les amis* /lez ami/, *vous avez* /vuz ave/ |
| *n* | /n/ | *un ami* /œ̃n ami/, *bon ami* /bɔn ami/ |
| *t* | /t/ | *un petit hôtel* /œ̃ pətit otɛl/ |
| *d* | /t/ | *un grand hôtel* /œ̃ gʁɑ̃t otɛl/ |

*Ils ont* has an *s*, so it says /ilz ɔ̃/ (they have). Learn *vous avez* (you have), *ils ont* and *nous avons* (we have) as three sound patterns now. The verb *avoir* comes in lesson 7.

### Where liaison is required, forbidden or optional

The OQLF sorts liaison into three groups ([OQLF: contexts of liaison](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/la-prononciation/liaisons)). For A1 you need the common cases.

**Required.** Make the link in these cases:

1. A small word before a noun: *un ami* /œ̃n ami/, *les amis*, *des amis* /dez ami/, *mes amis* (my friends).
2. A pronoun before its verb: *vous avez*, *ils ont*, *nous avons* /nuz avɔ̃/.
3. Most one-syllable prepositions: *dans une* /dɑ̃z yn/, *en été* /ɑ̃n ete/ (in summer).
4. A short adjective before the noun: *un bon ami*, *un petit hôtel* /œ̃ pətit otɛl/.

**Forbidden.** Never link in these cases:

1. After *et*: *un garçon et un homme* /œ̃ gaʁsɔ̃ e œ̃n ɔm/. There is no /t/ at the end of *et*.
2. Before an h aspiré: *les héros*, *les hamburgers*.
3. After a singular noun: *le chat est ici*, with no /t/ between *chat* and *est*.

**Optional.** In the other cases the OQLF says you may or may not link. More speakers link in careful speech, and in everyday speech people almost never do ([OQLF: optional liaisons](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/23550/la-prononciation/liaisons/contextes-de-liaisons-facultatives)). A plain rule for now: make the required links, skip the optional ones. Nobody will fault you for it.

### Oddities worth knowing

*Femme* is said /fam/, not /fɛm/. *Œuf* is /œf/ and *œufs* is /ø/ with the *f* and *s* silent, so *un œuf* and *des œufs* /dez ø/ change sound in the plural. *Les gens* /le ʒɑ̃/ (people) has no /z/ before a consonant.

## Hear it

Listen for the /z/ and /n/ links that appear in the first block, and for the clean break in the second.

```fr
Un ami, une amie. | A friend (male), a friend (female).
Les amis, des amis. | The friends, some friends.
Vous avez. Ils ont. Nous avons. | You have. They have. We have.
En été, dans une rue. | In summer, in a street.
Un bon ami, un petit hôtel. | A good friend, a small hotel.
```

```fr
Un garçon et un homme. | A boy and a man.
Le héros, les héros. | The hero, the heroes.
Un hamburger. | A hamburger.
Un œuf, des œufs. | An egg, some eggs.
```

A hotel sign and a métro announcement (our own examples) show the same patterns in short lines.

```fr
Affiche: Bienvenue à l'hôtel. | Sign: Welcome to the hotel.
Annonce: Mesdames et messieurs, bonsoir. | Announcement: Ladies and gentlemen, good evening.
Annonce: Vous êtes ici. | Announcement: You are here.
```

## Try it

1. How do you pronounce the *s* of *les amis*? Does *les garçons* have a link?
2. Write the sentence with elision: *je aime*, *le hôtel*, *de accord*.
3. *Le héros* and *l'homme*: which one has an h aspiré, and how can you tell?
4. Does *un garçon et un homme* link *et* to *un*?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. /z/: *les amis* is /lez ami/. *Les garçons* has no link because *garçons* starts with a consonant.
2. *J'aime*, *l'hôtel*, *d'accord*. (*J'aime* means "I like", and you meet it in stage 3.)
3. *Le héros* has the h aspiré: you say *le*, not *l'*, and there is no /z/ in *les héros*. *L'homme* has an h muet.
4. No. *Et* never links.

</details>

## Say it

```fr
Nous avons un ami. | We have a friend.
Vous avez un bon hôtel. | You have a good hotel.
Ils ont des œufs. | They have some eggs.
C'est l'hôtel d'un ami. | It's a friend's hotel.
Merci beaucoup, à bientôt. | Thank you very much, see you soon.
```

## Common mistakes

Linking after *et*, as in "ay-t un". *Et* is always followed by a break: *un garçon et un homme*.

Treating every h the same. An h muet allows elision and liaison (*l'homme*, *les hommes*), and an h aspiré blocks both (*le héros*).

Linking everything, in an effort to sound French. Required links are enough at this level, and the optional ones are often skipped in real speech.

## In Quebec

Careful speech in Quebec keeps liaison as in France. The OQLF says liaisons are more frequent in careful speech and almost never made in familiar speech, which holds for French everywhere ([OQLF: optional liaisons](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/23550/la-prononciation/liaisons/contextes-de-liaisons-facultatives)). [Casual questions in Quebec](../03-verbs-and-questions/10-quebec-casual-questions.md) shows how spoken forms get shorter. The h aspiré list is the same as in France.

## Key terms

- **Elision**: the loss of a final vowel before a vowel, shown by an apostrophe: *l'hôtel*.
- **Liaison**: a silent final consonant that is pronounced before a vowel at the start of the next word: *les amis* /lez ami/.
- **Enchaînement**: the sliding of a pronounced final consonant onto the vowel of the next word: *il est* /i lɛ/.
- **H muet**: a silent *h* that lets elision and liaison happen.
- **H aspiré**: a silent *h* that blocks elision and liaison.
