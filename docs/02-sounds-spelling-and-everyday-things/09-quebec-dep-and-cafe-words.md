# Quebec words: dépanneur and café

After this lesson you can name the Quebec words for a corner shop, money, soft drinks, meals and email, say which ones are casual, and understand them in a shop or on a menu.

## The idea

Quebec French and the French of France share one grammar and one spelling. They also share most of their words. A small group of everyday words about shops, money and food differ, and you will hear them on your first day in Montreal. This lesson sorts them by register: **neutral** words that you can say anywhere, and **casual** words that people say in speech and that you recognise first. Each fact below comes from the Office québécois de la langue française (OQLF), the Quebec language office, or from [Usito](https://usito.usherbrooke.ca/), a free dictionary of Quebec French made at the Université de Sherbrooke. Dictionary labels and OQLF advice can change, so the checks are dated 2026.

### The corner shop: un dépanneur

A *dépanneur* is a small neighbourhood grocery shop that stays open later than other shops. Usito labels this sense as Quebec and Canadian, and says the OQLF has made the use official ([Usito: dépanneur](https://usito.usherbrooke.ca/définitions/dépanneur)). It is a neutral word you can say and write.

### Money: une piasse, une cenne

*Une piasse* /pjas/ is a casual word for a Canadian dollar. Usito marks it as Quebec and familiar, and gives the example *Je te dois vingt piasses* (I owe you twenty dollars) ([Usito: piasse](https://usito.usherbrooke.ca/définitions/piasse)). The word is a spelling of *piastre*, an older name for a unit of money. *Une cenne* /sɛn/ is the casual word for one cent, also familiar and usually feminine in Quebec ([Usito: cenne](https://usito.usherbrooke.ca/définitions/cenne)). So *deux piasses cinquante* is two dollars fifty in speech, and *deux dollars cinquante* is the standard form. Say *dollar* when you write and when you talk to a stranger, and understand *piasse*.

### Drinks and snacks

*Une liqueur* is what many people in Quebec call a soft drink. In standard French the word means a sweet alcoholic drink, so the Quebec use is a surprise. The OQLF says *liqueur* is very common in familiar speech in Quebec, and gives *boisson gazeuse* for the general term ([OQLF: boisson gazeuse](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/fiche-gdt/fiche/8870732/boisson-gazeuse)). Usito marks the soft-drink sense as Quebec, familiar and criticized ([Usito: liqueur](https://usito.usherbrooke.ca/définitions/liqueur)). Order it, hear it, and know that a menu may say *boisson gazeuse*.

*Un breuvage* means any drink in Quebec. Usito labels it Quebec, familiar and criticized, and says it stayed alive partly through the English word "beverage" ([Usito: breuvage](https://usito.usherbrooke.ca/définitions/breuvage)). The OQLF prefers *boisson* ([OQLF: boisson](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/fiche-gdt/fiche/8872875/boisson)). Use *boisson* in writing, and recognise *breuvage* on a menu.

*Une crème glacée* is ice cream. The OQLF says the term became established in Quebec, and that *glace* and *crème glacée* are both used in Quebec and in Europe ([OQLF: crème glacée](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/fiche-gdt/fiche/8362247/creme-glacee)).

*Un sous-marin* is a long sandwich on an elongated bun. The word is a translation of "submarine", and the OQLF says it is accepted because it fills a gap in French ([OQLF: sous-marin](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/fiche-gdt/fiche/8870068/sous-marin)).

*Une poutine* is a Quebec dish: fries with cheese curds and brown gravy (Usito: [poutine](https://usito.usherbrooke.ca/définitions/poutine)). *Une patate* is an everyday word for a potato. Usito gives *pomme de terre* (potato, literally "apple of the earth") as its synonym and says labels on food packages prefer *pomme de terre* ([Usito: patate](https://usito.usherbrooke.ca/définitions/patate)). You will meet both words, and both are fine in speech.

### Meals: déjeuner, dîner, souper

Meal names are the biggest trap, because the same word means a different meal. This table gives the usual senses, from the OQLF term records for *déjeuner*, *dîner* and *souper*.

| Meal | Quebec | France |
|---|---|---|
| morning | *le déjeuner* | *le petit-déjeuner* |
| midday | *le dîner* | *le déjeuner* |
| evening | *le souper* | *le dîner* |

([OQLF: déjeuner](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/fiche-gdt/fiche/8368956/dejeuner), [OQLF: dîner](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/fiche-gdt/fiche/26511882/diner), [OQLF: souper](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/fiche-gdt/fiche/8872574/souper)). The OQLF adds that *petit-déjeuner* is used in Quebec in careful speech and in material for tourists, and that in France *souper* usually means a light meal very late in the evening. In Canada, *dîner* for the evening meal is mostly formal. If a Quebec friend invites you to *souper*, you are being invited to the evening meal. If a French friend invites you to *dîner*, expect the evening as well.

### Email: un courriel

*Un courriel* is an email message. The OQLF lists *courriel* among its preferred terms with *courrier électronique* (electronic mail), and advises against *email* and *mail* in French ([OQLF: courriel](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/fiche-gdt/fiche/8353974/courriel)). The France-side commission also recommended *courriel* in 2003. Use *courriel* in Quebec, on forms and in writing.

## Hear it

Listen for the casual word *piasse*, which is marked as casual. The first dialogue is a late-evening dépanneur, and the owner is Madame Gagnon (60). A student speaks to her with *vous*. In the second, they know each other and use *tu*.

```fr
Étudiant: Bonsoir, madame. Je voudrais une liqueur et une crème glacée, s'il vous plaît. | Good evening. I would like a soft drink and an ice cream, please.
Mme-Gagnon: Voilà. Ça fait deux piasses cinquante. {qc} | Here you are. That comes to two dollars fifty.
Étudiant: Voilà. Merci beaucoup, madame. | Here you are. Thank you very much.
Mme-Gagnon: Merci, bonne soirée! | Thank you, have a good evening!
```

```fr
Mme-Gagnon: Salut! Ça va? Une liqueur, comme toujours? | Hi! How's it going? A soft drink, as usual?
Étudiant: Oui, merci. Et un sandwich, s'il te plaît. | Yes, thanks. And a sandwich, please.
Mme-Gagnon: Ça fait cinq piasses. {qc} | That comes to five dollars.
Étudiant: Voilà. À bientôt! | Here you are. See you soon!
```

Meals and email, in short lines:

```fr
Le déjeuner, le dîner, le souper. | Breakfast, lunch, dinner (Quebec).
Une poutine, une patate. | A poutine, a potato.
Un courriel, un message. | An email, a message.
```

## Try it

1. A friend in Montreal says *Voilà le dépanneur.* What kind of place is it?
2. Say the standard form of *trois piasses*.
3. A Quebec friend says "Le souper est à six heures." Which meal is it? In France, which word would you use for the same meal?
4. Which word do you use on a form for the email address: *email* or *courriel*?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. A small neighbourhood grocery shop that stays open late.
2. *Trois dollars.*
3. The evening meal. In France, *le dîner*.
4. *Courriel*, which is the OQLF's preferred term in Quebec.

## Common mistakes

Taking *dîner* to mean the evening meal in Quebec. It is the midday meal, and the evening meal is *souper*.

Writing *piasse* in an email to a stranger. It is casual, so write *dollar* in writing.

Thinking *liqueur* is alcoholic everywhere. In Quebec it is also a soft drink, and in France it is a sweet alcoholic drink. Listen to the context.

## In Quebec

This whole lesson is about Quebec words. Casual Quebec forms are *piasse*, *cenne*, *liqueur* and *breuvage*. The neutral ones are *dépanneur*, *courriel*, *crème glacée*, *sous-marin*, *poutine* and *patate*. A word with a label can still be fine in conversation, and people use all of them. A Quebec word is not wrong French: it is a choice about register.
