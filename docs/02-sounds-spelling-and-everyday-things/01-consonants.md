# French consonants

<span class="tag tag-sounds">Sounds</span> <span class="tag tag-a1">A1</span>

After this lesson you can say the French consonants /p b t d k g f v s z ʃ ʒ m n l ɲ/, read the common spellings of *c*, *g*, *s*, *ch* and *qu*, and tell which final consonants you pronounce.

## The idea

A **consonant** is a sound where the air is blocked or squeezed somewhere in your mouth or throat, as in /p/ (lips closed) or /s/ (tongue near the teeth). A vowel leaves the air free. Most French consonants are close to English ones. This lesson covers the ones that are not, and the spelling rules that tell you which sound a letter makes. The French /ʁ/ and the sounds /j w ɥ/ come in the next lesson.

### Voiced and voiceless pairs

Many consonants come in pairs that differ in one way. Put two fingers on your throat. For a **voiced** consonant your throat buzzes, and for a **voiceless** consonant it does not.

| Voiceless | Voiced | How you make it |
|---|---|---|
| /p/ | /b/ | both lips |
| /t/ | /d/ | tongue tip on the back of the upper teeth |
| /k/ | /g/ | back of the tongue against the soft palate |
| /f/ | /v/ | upper teeth on the lower lip |
| /s/ | /z/ | air squeezed between the tongue and the front teeth |
| /ʃ/ | /ʒ/ | like /s/ and /z/, with the lips pushed forward |

The symbol /ʃ/ is the *sh* of "shoe" and /ʒ/ is the *s* of "measure". French also has /m/, /n/ and /l/, close to English, and /ɲ/, the sound in the middle of "canyon", said as one movement.

### Three differences from English

1. **French /p/, /t/ and /k/ have no puff of air.** In English "pot" you push out a breath. Hold a sheet of paper in front of your mouth and say *pain* /pɛ̃/: the paper should hardly move. If it flies, relax the lips and say the sound with less air.
2. **French /t/ and /d/ use the teeth.** The tongue tip touches the back of the upper front teeth, not the ridge behind them. This is what makes *tout* /tu/ sound French.
3. **French /l/ is always light.** The tongue tip is up behind the upper teeth and the rest of the tongue is flat. English "ball" has a dark /l/ at the end, and *hôtel* /otɛl/ does not.

### Spelling: one letter, two sounds

Some letters change sound depending on the letter after them. Learn these four pairs.

| Letter | Sound | When | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| *c* | /k/ | before *a*, *o*, *u*, or a consonant | *café* /kafe/, *sac* /sak/, *clé* /kle/ |
| *c* | /s/ | before *e*, *i*, *y* | *merci* /mɛʁsi/ |
| *ç* | /s/ | before *a*, *o*, *u* | *garçon* /gaʁsɔ̃/, *leçon* /ləsɔ̃/ (lesson) |
| *g* | /g/ | before *a*, *o*, *u*, or a consonant | *garçon*, *grand* /gʁɑ̃/ |
| *g* | /ʒ/ | before *e*, *i*, *y* | *les gens* /le ʒɑ̃/ (people), *une page* /yn paʒ/ |
| *gu* | /g/ | before *e*, *i* (the *u* is silent) | *une guitare* /gitaʁ/ |
| *ge* | /ʒ/ | before *a*, *o*, *u* (the *e* is silent) | *un pigeon* /piʒɔ̃/ (pigeon) |
| *qu* | /k/ | the *u* is silent | *qui* /ki/ (who), *un kilo* /kilo/ (kilo; the letter *k* is also /k/) |

Letters that match one sound: *j* is /ʒ/ (*un jour* /ʒuʁ/), *ch* is /ʃ/ (*un chat* /ʃa/, *une chose* /ʃoz/), *ph* is /f/ (*une photo* /fɔto/), *th* is /t/ (*un thé* /te/, not English "th"), *gn* is /ɲ/ (*un signe* /siɲ/ (sign)), and *h* is never pronounced (*l'hôtel*, *l'heure*).

The letter *s* is /s/ at the start of a word (*un sac*) and when doubled (*dessus* /dəsy/), but /z/ between two vowels (*une chose*, *la maison* /la mɛzɔ̃/). A single *z* is always /z/.

The letter *x* is usually /ks/ (*un taxi* /taksi/ (taxi)) or /gz/ (*un exemple* /ɛgzɑ̃pl/). A few words end in *-ing*, borrowed from English, where *ng* is /ŋ/, as in *le camping* /kɑ̃piŋ/ (camping). That sound is not a normal French one, so it only turns up in these loanwords.

### Final consonants

Most final consonants are silent: *petit* /pəti/, *trop* /tʁo/, *grand* /gʁɑ̃/, *dans* /dɑ̃/, *chez* /ʃe/. The exceptions are easy to remember with the word CaReFuL: the letters *c*, *r*, *f* and *l* are often pronounced at the end of a word, as in *sac* /sak/, *bonjour* /bɔ̃ʒuʁ/, *hôtel* /otɛl/ and *neuf* /nœf/ (nine). Watch out: *blanc* /blɑ̃/ and *banc* /bɑ̃/ are exceptions, and the verb ending *-er* says /e/ with no *r*. When a word has an exception, you learn it with the word.

## Hear it

Listen for the missing puff of air on /p/, /t/ and /k/, and for the buzz in the second word of each pair.

```fr
Pain, bain. | Bread, bath.
Tout, doux. | All, soft.
Car, gare. | Because, station.
Fin, vin. | End, wine.
Chou, joue. | Cabbage, cheek.
```

The next lines have objects on a desk. Say each article with its noun: the article tells you the gender, which [Gender and articles](04-gender-and-articles.md) explains.

```fr
Une table, une chaise, un bureau. | A table, a chair, a desk.
Un livre, un stylo, un sac. | A book, a pen, a bag.
Un téléphone, un ordinateur, une photo. | A phone, a computer, a photo.
Une clé, un chat, une chose. | A key, a cat, a thing.
```

## Try it

1. Say the sound of *c* in *café*, *merci*, *clé* and *garçon*.
2. Say the sound of *g* in *grand*, *les gens* and *une guitare*.
3. Which final consonants are pronounced: *sac*, *petit*, *hôtel*, *chez*?
4. Do the paper test: say *pain*, *table* and *café* with a sheet of paper in front of your mouth.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. *Café* /k/, *merci* /s/, *clé* /k/, *garçon* /s/ (the cedilla).
2. *Grand* /g/, *les gens* /ʒ/, *une guitare* /g/ (the *u* is silent).
3. *Sac* (/k/) and *hôtel* (/l/). *Petit* and *chez* are silent.
4. The paper should barely move on /p/, /t/ and /k/. If it moves a lot, say them again with less air.

</details>

## Say it

```fr
C'est une table. C'est un ordinateur. | It's a table. It's a computer.
Voilà un stylo et un livre. | Here is a pen and a book.
Un chat, un sac, une clé. | A cat, a bag, a key.
Une photo et un garçon. | A photo and a boy.
```

## Common mistakes

Puffing air on /p/, /t/ and /k/, as in English "pot". Relax and use less air: the paper should hardly move.

Saying *th* as in English "think" in *thé*. The letters *th* are /t/ in French.

Pronouncing final consonants in *petit*, *trop*, *grand* and *chez*. Learn the CaReFuL letters, and treat the rest as silent unless a word is an exception.

## In Quebec

You already know that Quebec French adds a small hiss to /t/ and /d/ before /i/ and /y/, as in [t͡sy] for *tu* ([OQLF: l'affrication](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/24467/la-prononciation/phenomenes-phonetiques/laffrication)). Nothing else in this lesson changes. The tongue-on-teeth /t/ is the base for it.

## Key terms

- **Consonant**: a sound made by blocking or squeezing the air in the mouth or throat.
- **Voiced**: a sound where the throat buzzes, such as /b/ or /z/.
- **Voiceless**: a sound with no buzz in the throat, such as /p/ or /s/.
