# Hearing numbers and prices

<span class="tag tag-listen">Listen</span> <span class="tag tag-a1">A1</span>

After this lesson you can understand prices, change and discounts when someone says them at normal speed, take down a phone number, and write a price the French way.

Before this: [Numbers from 0 to 100](06-numbers-0-100.md), [Ordering at a café](08-ordering-at-a-cafe.md), [Quebec words: dépanneur and café](09-quebec-dep-and-cafe-words.md). You need: a pencil and paper, and headphones if you have them.

## The idea

Saying a number is easier than hearing one. A person speaking at normal speed does not pause between *quatre-vingt-quinze* and the next word, and prices come in the middle of a sentence. This lesson trains your ear on short real-life scenes. Each one has the price or number you must catch, so listen first with the translation hidden and write the numbers on paper.

### Prices in speech and in writing

Spoken, a price has the dollars, the word *dollars* and the cents: 4,95 $ is *quatre dollars quatre-vingt-quinze*. When the cents are zero, you leave them out: 6,00 $ is *six dollars*. In writing, the OQLF places the dollar sign after the number, with a space between them and a comma as the decimal sign, so you write 4,95 $ and not $4.95 ([OQLF: écriture des sommes d'argent](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/21584/la-typographie/nombres/ecriture-des-sommes-dargent)).

### Words for money and shopping

*Cher* means "expensive", and *chère* is the feminine form, as in *une chambre chère*. *Bon marché* means "inexpensive" and never changes. *Gratuit* means "free of charge". A *rabais* is a discount, and *en solde* means "on sale". *La monnaie* is change, the coins and bills you get back. *Un total* is the final amount. You ask about a price with *C'est combien?* from the café lesson, or with *Ça coûte combien?*, a second chunk that you can learn whole. The verb *coûter* ("to cost") is explained later.

### Taxes

In Quebec, two sales taxes are added to many purchases: the *TPS* (*taxe sur les produits et services*, goods and services tax), which the federal government collects, and the *TVQ* (*taxe de vente du Québec*, Quebec sales tax), which the province collects. The OQLF's term record says the TVQ is added to the TPS ([OQLF: taxe de vente du Québec](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/fiche-gdt/fiche/8364127/taxe-de-vente-du-quebec)). Rates change, so this book gives none: look at the receipt. You may hear *avec les taxes* or see the two initials on a bill.

### Phone numbers

A phone number is a *numéro de téléphone*. People say it digit by digit, or in pairs, or in a mix. For *514 555 0123*, you might hear *cinq, un, quatre* for the area code, then *cinq cinq cinq* and *zéro un deux trois*. Some speakers give pairs, such as *zéro un, vingt-trois*. Catch each digit and write it as you hear it. If you miss one, say *Pardon?* from stage 1 and listen again.

### Paying

The usual choice at a till is *Carte ou comptant?* (card or cash), and *comptant* means "in cash". A *carte de débit* is a debit card. *Le total* is what you pay.

## Hear it

Read nothing yet. Play each scene with the translation hidden and write the numbers. All three scenes use *vous*, because they are with people you do not know. Our own examples.

The first scene is a fruit stall at Marché Jean-Talon in Montréal.

```fr
Vous: Bonjour. C'est combien, les pommes? | Hello. How much are the apples?
Marchand: Quatre dollars quatre-vingt-quinze. | Four dollars ninety-five.
Vous: C'est un peu cher. Et les œufs? | That's a bit expensive. And the eggs?
Marchand: Les œufs, six dollars. | The eggs, six dollars.
Vous: Je voudrais des œufs et des pommes, s'il vous plaît. | I would like some eggs and some apples, please.
Marchand: Ça fait dix dollars quatre-vingt-quinze. Vous avez la monnaie? | That comes to ten ninety-five. Do you have change?
Vous: Non, voilà vingt dollars. | No, here is twenty dollars.
Marchand: Merci. Voilà la monnaie : neuf dollars cinq. | Thank you. Here is your change: nine dollars five.
```

The second scene is at the till of a pharmacy.

```fr
Caissière: Bonjour, madame. Ça fait vingt-trois dollars soixante-quinze. | Hello, ma'am. That comes to twenty-three dollars seventy-five.
Vous: Voilà ma carte de débit. | Here is my debit card.
Caissière: Carte ou comptant? | Card or cash?
Vous: Carte, merci. | Card, thanks.
Caissière: Parfait. Bonne journée! | Perfect. Have a good day!
```

The third scene is a voicemail with a phone number and a sale.

```fr
Messagerie: Bonjour, c'est Marc. Tout est en solde, vingt pour cent de rabais. | Hello, it's Marc. Everything is on sale, twenty percent off.
Messagerie: Mon numéro est le cinq, un, quatre, cinq, cinq, cinq, zéro, un, deux, trois. | My number is five, one, four, five, five, five, zero, one, two, three.
Messagerie: C'est gratuit pour les amis. Merci, au revoir! | It's free for friends. Thank you, goodbye!
```

## Try it

Play the scenes again, with the answers closed.

1. Scene one: how much are the apples and the eggs? What is the total, and how much change does the vendor give back?
2. Scene two: what is the total? Does the customer pay by card or in cash?
3. Scene three: what is the discount? Write down the phone number in digits.
4. Write these two prices the French way: *douze dollars cinquante* and *quatre-vingt-dix-neuf dollars*.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. Apples 4,95 $, eggs 6,00 $. The total is 10,95 $. The change is 9,05 $ (neuf dollars cinq).
2. 23,75 $, by card (*carte*).
3. Twenty percent (*vingt pour cent*). The number is 514 555 0123.
4. 12,50 $ and 99 $ (or 99,00 $).

</details>

## Say it

```fr
C'est combien? Ça coûte combien? | How much is it? How much does it cost?
Quatre dollars quatre-vingt-quinze. | Four ninety-five.
C'est cher. C'est bon marché. | It's expensive. It's cheap.
Carte ou comptant? Carte, merci. | Card or cash? Card, thanks.
Mon numéro, c'est le cinq, un, quatre. | My number is five, one, four.
```

## Common mistakes

Writing the price the English way, $4.95. In French writing, put the sign after the number, with a space, and use a comma: 4,95 $.

Hearing *quatre-vingt-quinze* as 80 plus 5. It is 4 times 20 plus 15, which is 95.

Mixing up *soixante* with *cinquante* in a fast price. Listen for the vowel: /swa/ against /sɛ̃/.

## In Quebec

Amounts are written 4,95 $, with a comma and a space before the dollar sign, as the OQLF page above says. You may hear *piasse* for a dollar, and *cenne* for a cent: both are casual. The TPS and the TVQ are two separate taxes, federal and provincial, so check the receipt for the total.
