# Days, dates and big numbers

<span class="tag tag-words">Words</span> <span class="tag tag-a1">A1</span>

After this lesson you can name the days and months, say and write a date, say numbers from 100 to a million, and say a year.

Before this: [Numbers from 0 to 100](../02-sounds-spelling-and-everyday-things/06-numbers-0-100.md), [Gender and articles](../02-sounds-spelling-and-everyday-things/04-gender-and-articles.md), [Regular -er verbs](02-regular-er-verbs.md). You need: nothing.

## The idea

A date has three parts: a day of the week, a number and a month. French writes the number first, then the month, then the year, and it does not capitalise the days and the months. We start with the words, then the date pattern, then the big numbers.

| *jeudi* | /ʒødi/ | *avril* | /avʁil/ |
| *vendredi* | /vɑ̃dʁədi/ | *mai* | /mɛ/ |
| *samedi* | /samdi/ | *juin* | /ʒɥɛ̃/ |
| *dimanche* | /dimɑ̃ʃ/ | *juillet* | /ʒɥijɛ/ |
| | | *août* | /ut/ |
| | | *septembre* | /sɛptɑ̃bʁ/ |

| | | *novembre* | /nɔvɑ̃bʁ/ |
| | | *décembre* | /desɑ̃bʁ/ |

The days are all masculine. A day with no article means the coming one: *lundi* means "this Monday" or "on Monday". A day with *le* means "every": *le lundi* means "on Mondays". So *Je travaille lundi* is one day, and *Je travaille le lundi* is a habit.

A **week** is *une semaine*, a **month** is *un mois*, and a **year** is *un an* or *une année*. Use *an* with a number (*j'ai vingt ans*), and *année* when you think of the year as a stretch of time. Three more time words are *aujourd'hui* "today", *demain* "tomorrow" and *hier* "yesterday".

### Saying and writing a date

To say a date, use *le*, the number, and the month: *le quatorze novembre*. For the first of the month, use *premier* (*le premier mars*), and write it *le 1er mars*. For the other days, use the plain number. The question is *Quelle est la date?* and one answer is *On est le 14 novembre* or *C'est le 14 novembre.*

When you write a date in a letter or an email, the OQLF gives the form *le 14 novembre 2026*, with the month in letters, in lowercase. With a day of the week, put the day first: *samedi 14 novembre 2026*, with *le* before it in a sentence ([OQLF: écriture des dates](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/21640/la-typographie/nombres/ecriture-des-dates)). The OQLF also gives an all-number form for tables, forms and records: year, then month, then day, with hyphens, so *2026-11-14*. It prefers a four-digit year, and it discourages the slash. The OQLF page does not cover the day-of-week order, which is the usual French order. You saw this form in [Filling in forms](../02-sounds-spelling-and-everyday-things/11-filling-in-forms.md).

To count the order of days or things, French has ordinal numbers. *Premier* is "first" (feminine *première*), and *deuxième* is "second". For the others, add *-ième* to the number: *troisième*, *quatrième*. A final *e*, as in *quatre*, drops before the ending. Two numbers change their spelling: *cinq* becomes *cinquième* and *neuf* becomes *neuvième*.

*Mon anniversaire* is "my birthday": *Mon anniversaire est le 3 mai.* The word *les vacances* (always plural) means "holidays" or "vacation".

### The weekend

The weekend is *la fin de semaine* in Quebec, and *le week-end* in France. In its entry for *fin de semaine*, the OQLF marks it as the preferred term in Quebec and Canada, and says *week-end* is a borrowing from English that is often criticised in Quebec ([OQLF: fin de semaine](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/redirection/ficheuid/8361442)). You will see *week-end* in French texts from elsewhere, and people understand both. This book uses *fin de semaine* first.

### Big numbers

Here are the new pieces. After 100, you say the hundreds, then the rest.

| Number | In words |
|---|---|
| 100 | *cent* |
| 101 | *cent un* |
| 200 | *deux cents* |
| 201 | *deux cent un* |
| 365 | *trois cent soixante-cinq* |
| 1 000 | *mille* |
| 1 250 | *mille deux cent cinquante* |
| 3 500 | *trois mille cinq cents* |
| 100 000 | *cent mille* |
| 1 000 000 | *un million* |
| 2 000 000 | *deux millions* |

Three rules cover most of it. *Cent* takes an *s* when it is multiplied (*deux cents*) and has nothing after it. If a number follows, there is no *s*: *deux cent un*. *Mille* never takes an *s*: *deux mille*. *Million* is a noun, so it takes an *s* in the plural and needs *de* before a noun: *un million de dollars*. In the traditional spelling that this book uses, hyphens join only the numbers below 100 (*soixante-cinq*), and no hyphen goes around *et* (*vingt et un*, *cent trente et un*). A spelling reform in 1990 allows hyphens between all parts, as in *deux-cent-un*, and the OQLF accepts both ([OQLF: écriture des déterminants numéraux](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/23494/la-typographie/nombres/ecriture-des-determinants-numeraux)).

For a year, say the thousands and hundreds: *2026* is *deux mille vingt-six*, and *1999* is *mille neuf cent quatre-vingt-dix-neuf*. In writing, use figures, with all four digits.

## Hear it

In the first dialogue a landlord and a tenant use *vous*. In the second, two friends use *tu* and plan a party. The numbers and the price are invented. Listen for the big numbers and the months.

```fr
M-Bouchard: Vous cherchez une chambre? | Are you looking for a room?
Vous: Oui. Elle est libre quand? | Yes. When is it free?
M-Bouchard: Elle est libre le premier décembre. | It is free on the first of December.
Vous: Je reste un mois, jusqu'au 1er janvier. | I'm staying a month, until January 1st.
M-Bouchard: D'accord. Ça fait mille deux cents dollars. | Okay. That's one thousand two hundred dollars.

Léa: Quand est ton anniversaire? | When is your birthday?
Samir: Le 3 mai. Et toi? | May 3rd. And you?
Léa: Le 25 octobre. Aujourd'hui, on est jeudi. | October 25th. Today is Thursday.
Samir: Demain, c'est vendredi. On fait la fête samedi? | Tomorrow is Friday. Shall we party on Saturday?
Léa: Oui! Le samedi, je ne travaille pas. | Yes! On Saturdays, I don't work.
```

## Try it

1. Write in words: *101*, *280*, *1 250*, *2026*.
2. Say these dates in French: *le 14 novembre*, *le 1er mars*, *le 25 décembre*.
3. Write the date *25 décembre 2026* in letters-and-numbers form and in all-number form.
4. What is the difference between *Je travaille lundi* and *Je travaille le lundi*?
5. Say your own birthday, and say it as a sentence that starts with *Mon anniversaire est*

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. *cent un*, *deux cent quatre-vingts*, *mille deux cent cinquante*, *deux mille vingt-six*.
2. *Le quatorze novembre*, *le premier mars*, *le vingt-cinq décembre*.
3. *le 25 décembre 2026* and *2026-12-25*.
4. *Lundi* means one Monday, the next one. *Le lundi* means every Monday.
5. A model answer: *Mon anniversaire est le 3 mai.* Use your own date and month, with the month in lowercase.

</details>

## Say it

```fr
Aujourd'hui, on est le 14 novembre. | Today is November 14th.
Mon anniversaire est le premier mars. | My birthday is March 1st.
Le samedi, je fais les courses. | On Saturdays, I do the errands.
Ça fait mille deux cents dollars. | That's twelve hundred dollars.
Nous sommes en deux mille vingt-six. | It's 2026.
```

## Common mistakes

Capitalising days and months, as in English. French writes *lundi* and *mars* with a lowercase first letter, except at the start of a sentence.

Writing the month before the day, as in "November 14". French says *le 14 novembre*.

Adding an *s* to *mille*. It never changes: *deux mille*.

## In Quebec

*La fin de semaine* is the Quebec word for the weekend, and the OQLF prefers it to *week-end*. The all-number date, such as *2026-11-14*, is the form the OQLF gives for forms and records.
