# Regular -er verbs

<span class="tag tag-grammar">Grammar</span> <span class="tag tag-a1">A1</span>

After this lesson you can use about twenty regular verbs that end in *-er* with all the subject pronouns, say what you like and dislike, and use *on* to mean "we".

Before this: [Être and introducing yourself](../01-first-sounds-and-greetings/10-etre-and-introductions.md), [Gender and articles](../02-sounds-spelling-and-everyday-things/04-gender-and-articles.md), [Spelling rules and liaison](../02-sounds-spelling-and-everyday-things/03-spelling-sound-rules-and-liaison.md). You need: nothing.

## The idea

In [Être and introducing yourself](../01-first-sounds-and-greetings/10-etre-and-introductions.md) you learned that a verb changes with its subject. *Être* is irregular, so you learn its forms one by one. Most French verbs are **regular**, which means they follow a pattern, and the biggest group of regular verbs ends in *-er*. Once you know the pattern, every new *-er* verb is ready to use.

### The pattern

The **infinitive** is the form of the verb that you find in a dictionary and that means "to ...": *parler* "to speak". To conjugate an *-er* verb, take off the *-er*. What is left is the **stem**: *parl-*. Then add an ending for each subject.

| Pronoun | Ending | *parler* | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| *je* | *-e* | *je parle* | /ʒə paʁl/ |
| *tu* | *-es* | *tu parles* | /ty paʁl/ |
| *il / elle / on* | *-e* | *il parle* | /il paʁl/ |
| *nous* | *-ons* | *nous parlons* | /nu paʁlɔ̃/ |
| *vous* | *-ez* | *vous parlez* | /vu paʁle/ |
| *ils / elles* | *-ent* | *ils parlent* | /il paʁl/ |

Listen to the table. Four of the six forms sound the same, /paʁl/. The endings *-e*, *-es* and *-ent* are silent, so *je parle*, *tu parles*, *il parle* and *ils parlent* are the same sound. The two forms you can hear are *nous parlons* and *vous parlez*. This is why *ils parlent* and *il parle* are written differently and heard the same, and why spelling these endings correctly takes practice.

### Elision and liaison

Two things from the earlier sounds lessons apply here. First, **elision** (the dropped vowel of a short word): *je* becomes *j'* before a vowel sound, so *je aime* is written *j'aime* /ʒɛm/. Because *h* is silent in *habiter*, you also write *j'habite* /ʒabit/.

Second, **liaison** (a consonant that you hear between two words) links the pronoun to a verb that starts with a vowel sound. You hear /z/ in *nous aimons* /nuz ɛmɔ̃/, *vous habitez* /vuz abite/ and *ils habitent* /ilz abit/. A verb that starts with a consonant sound has no link: *ils parlent* /il paʁl/.

### On means "we"

*On* is a pronoun that, in everyday speech, means "we": *On habite à Montréal* means "We live in Montreal". It takes the same verb form as *il* and *elle*. *Nous* is the same meaning in a more formal voice, and you will see it in writing and hear it in careful speech. You will also hear *on* with a more general sense, close to English "one" or "people". For now you can use *on* for "we".

### Saying what you like

*Aimer* means "to like" or "to love". To say what you like, put the definite article before the noun: *J'aime le café*, *J'aime la cuisine*, *J'aime les voyages*. You use the article here even though English says "I like coffee" with no article. The stronger verbs are *adorer* "to love" and *détester* "to hate": *J'adore le vin*, *Je déteste les problèmes*. After *aimer* and *adorer* you can also put another verb in the infinitive: *J'adore voyager*, "I love travelling".

### Two spelling notes

Verbs in *-ger*, such as *manger* and *voyager*, keep an *e* before *-ons* so that the *g* stays soft: *nous mangeons* /nu mɑ̃ʒɔ̃/, *nous voyageons*. Without the *e*, the written form "mangons" would be said with a hard /g/. You only need to remember this for *nous*. Other spelling changes in some *-er* verbs come in later lessons.

## Hear it

Léa and Karim meet at a language exchange evening in a Montreal bar. They are the same age and use *tu*. Listen for the silent endings and for the /z/ links.

```fr
Léa: Salut, je m'appelle Léa. Tu parles français? | Hi, my name is Léa. Do you speak French?
Karim: Un peu. Je m'appelle Karim. J'habite à Montréal. | A little. My name is Karim. I live in Montreal.
Léa: Moi aussi, j'habite à Montréal. Tu travailles où? | Me too, I live in Montreal. Where do you work?
Karim: Je travaille à l'hôpital. Je suis infirmier. Et toi? | I work at the hospital. I'm a nurse. And you?
Léa: J'étudie à l'université. J'adore voyager et je magasine souvent. | I study at the university. I love to travel and I often go shopping.
Karim: Moi, j'aime marcher dans le parc. Nous cherchons un café? | Me, I like to walk in the park. Shall we look for a café?
Léa: Oui! Mes amis habitent près d'ici. On mange ici? | Yes! My friends live near here. Shall we eat here?
Karim: D'accord. J'adore le café, mais je déteste le thé. | Okay. I love coffee, but I hate tea.
```

The lines use *nous cherchons* for "we are looking for" and *on mange* for "we eat" or "shall we eat". Both mean "we". Notice that *marcher* and *voyager* are verbs in the infinitive after *aimer* and *adorer*.

## Try it

1. Write the correct form: *Nous ___ (parler) français.* *Elle ___ (travailler) à l'hôpital.* (*l'hôpital* is "the hospital".) *Vous ___ (habiter) à Toronto?* *Je ___ (étudier) le français.*
2. Write each phrase with elision: *je aime*, *je habite*, *je adore*.
3. Which of these has a /z/ link: *ils habitent* or *ils parlent*?
4. Say in French: "We live in Toronto" with *on*, and then with *nous*. Say what you like with *aimer* and a noun with its article.
5. *Ils téléphonent* and *il téléphone* sound the same. How would you hear which one is meant?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. *Nous parlons français.* *Elle travaille à l'hôpital.* *Vous habitez à Toronto?* *J'étudie le français.*
2. *J'aime*, *j'habite*, *j'adore*.
3. *Ils habitent* /ilz abit/. *Ils parlent* has no link.
4. *On habite à Toronto. Nous habitons à Toronto.* A model answer for the last part: *J'aime le café.*
5. You cannot hear it from the verb alone. You hear it from the pronoun, which is /il/ in both, or from the context, so you listen for other words such as *les amis* (plural).

</details>

```fr
Nous aimons le café. | We like coffee.
On mange ici? | Shall we eat here?
```

## Common mistakes

Saying the final *-ent* of *ils parlent*. The ending is silent, and the verb sounds like *il parle*.

Leaving out the article after *aimer*. French says *J'aime le café*, not "J'aime café".

Writing "mangons" for *nous*. Verbs in *-ger* need the *e*: *nous mangeons*.

## In Quebec

*Magasiner* means "to shop" (to go to stores to buy things). Usito labels it as Quebec and Canadian usage and defines it as making purchases in stores ([Usito: magasiner](https://usito.usherbrooke.ca/d%C3%A9finitions/magasiner)). You will hear it a lot in Montreal.

## Key terms

- **Regular verb**: a verb that follows a pattern of endings, so you can conjugate it once you know the pattern.
- **Infinitive**: the dictionary form of a verb, such as *parler* "to speak".
- **Stem**: the part of an infinitive that stays when you take off *-er*, such as *parl-*.
- **Elision**: the dropping of a vowel before another vowel sound, written with an apostrophe, as in *j'aime*.
- **Liaison**: a consonant that you hear between two words, as in *nous aimons*.
