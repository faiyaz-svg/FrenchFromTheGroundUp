# Texting to make plans

<span class="tag tag-write">Write</span> <span class="tag tag-a1">A1</span>

After this lesson you can read the common French texting abbreviations, write a short casual text to plan an evening, and end a message in the right way for a friend.

Before this: [Making plans](08-making-plans.md), [Telling the time](07-telling-time.md), [Days, dates and big numbers](06-days-dates-and-big-numbers.md). You need: a phone or a keyboard.

## The idea

A text message is short, quick and casual. People write it as they speak, and they cut words. This lesson shows what people cut, where it is fine, and where it is not. The rule of thumb is that texting abbreviations are for friends and family, and that you write full words with *vous* in a text to a manager, a client or an office.

### Words for the device and the message

A text message is *un texto*. The OQLF gives *texto* first among its preferred terms, with *message texte* ("mostly used in Quebec") and the borrowed *SMS*, which it accepts because it is well established ([OQLF: texto](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/fiche-gdt/fiche/26506622/texto)). The phone is *un cellulaire* in Quebec. Usito labels that sense as Quebec and Canadian and says it is the common word, and gives *portable* as a synonym ([Usito: cellulaire](https://usito.usherbrooke.ca/d%C3%A9finitions/cellulaire)). *Portable* is the word you will see in French from France. The OQLF calls the style of short, abbreviated messages *langage texto* or *langage SMS* ([OQLF: langage texto](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/fiche-gdt/fiche/8363780/langage-texto)).

### Common abbreviations

The abbreviations below are common in French texting language, and the Wikipédia page gives *slt* and *mdr* (the Vikidia page is a list of them: [Vikidia: liste d'abréviations SMS](https://fr.vikidia.org/wiki/Liste_d%27abr%C3%A9viations_SMS), [Wikipédia: langage SMS](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langage_SMS)). They are written as they are typed, with no italics, because they are spellings and not words.

| Text | Full form | Meaning | Register |
|---|---|---|---|
| `slt` | *salut* | hi, bye | casual |
| `bjr` | *bonjour* | hello | casual |
| `stp` | *s'il te plaît* | please (*tu*) | casual |
| `svp` | *s'il vous plaît* | please (*vous*) | informal |
| `dsl` | *désolé* | sorry | casual |
| `bcp` | *beaucoup* | a lot | casual |
| `mdr` | *mort de rire* | laughing out loud | informal |
| `a+` | *à plus* | see you later | casual |
| `tkt` | *t'inquiète* | don't worry | informal, recognise only |

Abbreviations vary by age, region and person. The lists agree on these, but a friend may use other forms, and it is fine to ask. Do not use them in a work email or a text to someone you address with *vous*.

### How to end a message

For a friend, you can end with *à plus tard* ("see you later"), *à tout à l'heure* ("see you in a bit", later the same day), or *à samedi* ("see you Saturday"), with the day you will meet. Friends and family add *bises* (kisses, on the cheek) or *bisous* (little kisses). Both are casual and affectionate, and you use them with *tu* only.

### Punctuation in a text

In ordinary French writing there is no space before *?* and *!* in this book, and in a text you can drop the space the same way. The OQLF's advice for social networks says that where a non-breaking space is not available, you leave the space out so that the mark never starts a line, It says that you may repeat *!* or *?* for effect (*!!!*) but should not overdo it ([OQLF: typographie et ponctuation, réseaux sociaux](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/25377/la-redaction-et-la-communication/redaction-dans-les-reseaux-sociaux/typographie-et-ponctuation)).

## Hear it

Léa invites Marc to the movies, he is busy on Saturday, and they move it to Sunday. They use *tu*. This is the exchange in full words, so you can hear it.

```fr
Léa: Salut Marc! Ça te dit, un film samedi soir? | Hi Marc! Do you feel like a movie on Saturday night?
Marc: Salut! Désolé, samedi, je ne peux pas. Je travaille. | Hi! Sorry, I can't on Saturday. I'm working.
Léa: Pas de problème. Dimanche, ça marche? | No problem. Does Sunday work?
Marc: Oui, je veux bien! À quelle heure? | Yes, I'd like to! What time?
Léa: À 19 h 30, devant le cinéma? | At 7:30 in front of the movie theatre?
Marc: Ça marche. À dimanche! Bisous. | That works. See you Sunday! Kisses.
```

Here is how the same exchange can look as texts between friends. The abbreviations are all from the table.

| From | Message |
|---|---|
| Léa | `Slt Marc! Ça te dit, un film samedi soir?` |
| Marc | `Slt! Dsl, samedi, je ne peux pas. Je travaille.` |
| Léa | `Pas de problème. Dimanche, ça marche?` |
| Marc | `Oui, je veux bien! À quelle heure?` |
| Léa | `19 h 30, devant le cinéma?` |
| Marc | `Ça marche. A+! Bisous` |

### Writing task

Write five short text messages to a friend to plan next Saturday. Use 40 to 60 words in total. In the five messages, you should:

1. propose something with *tu veux* or *ça te dit*,
2. ask what time or where,
3. accept or refuse (with a reason if you refuse),
4. give a time in the form *19 h 30*,
5. end with *à samedi* or *à plus tard*.

Use at least two abbreviations from the table, and write a negative sentence once. A model answer follows, written in full words. In a real text, a friend might send `Slt` for *Salut*, `Dsl` for *Désolé* and `A+` for *À plus*.

```fr
Salut Samir! Ça te dit, un film samedi soir? | Hi Samir! Do you feel like a movie on Saturday night?
Tu es libre à quelle heure? Et on se retrouve où? | What time are you free? And where do we meet?
Je ne peux pas avant 20 h, désolé. Je travaille. | I can't before 8 p.m., sorry. I'm working.
Ça marche! On se retrouve à 20 h 30 devant le cinéma? | That works! Shall we meet at 8:30 p.m. in front of the movie theatre?
Super! À plus, à samedi! Bisous. | Great! See you later, see you Saturday! Kisses.
```

## Say it

Read your five messages out loud, as you would say them, and then these lines.


## Common mistakes

Using texting abbreviations with *vous*. They are for friends and family. At work, write full words.

Putting a space before *?* and *!*. In French text in this book, write *Tu viens?*, with no space.

Using *bisous* with a manager or a stranger. Keep it for family and close friends.

## In Quebec

The OQLF prefers *texto* and says *message texte* is mainly used in Quebec, and Usito marks *cellulaire* as the common Quebec and Canadian word for the phone. Quebec texters also write spoken forms such as *chu* for *je suis*. A study of Quebec text messages found that people spell these forms in different ways (*chu*, `shu`, `chui`) and that there is no shared standard ([Journal of French Language Studies](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-french-language-studies/article/ecrire-son-vernaculaire-variation-et-normes-communautaires-dans-les-messages-textes-en-francais-quebecois/2E6723394C6DFCBF39026F45350A2C0A)). Recognise them, and see [Casual questions in Quebec](10-quebec-casual-questions.md). Write standard French when you are unsure.

## Key terms

- **Abbreviation**: a shortened form of a word, such as `dsl` for *désolé*.
- **Register**: how formal or casual language is, for example casual with a friend and formal with a client.
