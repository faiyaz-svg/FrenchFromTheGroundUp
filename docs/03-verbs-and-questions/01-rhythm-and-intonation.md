# Rhythm and intonation

<span class="tag tag-sounds">Sounds</span> <span class="tag tag-a1">A1</span>

After this lesson you can say a short French sentence in rhythm groups, make a yes or no question rise and a statement fall, and put emphasis on one word.

Before this: [How French spelling, sounds and rhythm work](../01-first-sounds-and-greetings/01-french-sound-map.md), [Spelling rules and liaison](../02-sounds-spelling-and-everyday-things/03-spelling-sound-rules-and-liaison.md). You need: nothing.

## The idea

Lesson 1 said that French has no stress inside a word and that the voice lifts at the end of a group. This lesson shows what a group is and what the voice does around it. No new sound is taught. The work is the melody of the sentence, which is what makes you sound like you are speaking French and not reading it.

### Rhythm groups

A **rhythm group** (*un groupe rythmique*) is a short stretch of words said together as one unit, with no break inside it. The OQLF describes the main accent of a French sentence as falling on the last syllable of each group of words that go together, and that accent marks where one idea ends ([OQLF: l'accent tonique et l'accent expressif](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/24511/la-prononciation/notions-de-base-en-phonetique/laccent-tonique-et-laccent-expressif)). Everything before the last syllable of the group is said with an even beat.

French is also **syllable-timed**: each syllable takes about the same time, so a group of six syllables lasts about twice as long as a group of three. English leans on a stress every few syllables, and French does not ([SFU French linguistics notes](https://www.sfu.ca/fren270/Phonologie/page4_10.html)). To feel the difference, tap your finger once per syllable while you say *je suis de Toronto*: six taps, all the same.

Here are the groups of a few phrases, marked with a slash. Say each group in one breath.

| Phrase | Groups | Syllables |
|---|---|---|
| *Tu vas bien?* | *tu vas bien* | 3 |
| *Oui, ça va.* | *oui / ça va* | 1 + 2 |
| *Je suis de Toronto, et vous?* | *je suis de Toronto / et vous* | 6 + 2 |
| *Il y a un café ici.* | *il y a un café ici* | 8 |

### The silent e inside a group

In [The e vowels](../01-first-sounds-and-greetings/04-vowels-e-family.md) you met /ə/, the weak vowel of *je*, *le* and *de*. In careful speech it is said, and in everyday speech it often nearly disappears in short words, such as the *e* of *de* in *tu es de Montréal*. The final *e* of a word like *chaise* or *porte* is silent. So *une chaise* /yn ʃɛz/ has two syllables, and you do not add a vowel after the *z*. In the same way *une porte* has two syllables and ends on the *t*. Dropping a weak *e* makes a group flow, so you do not need to worry when you hear a short word almost vanish.

### Rising and falling

Your voice also moves up and down across the group, and this movement is called **intonation**. Three patterns cover most of what you will say.

1. A statement falls at the end: *Ça va.* *Je suis de Toronto.*
2. A yes or no question rises at the end: *Ça va? Tu es de Montréal?* The words stay the same as the statement. Only the voice changes. Delattre described this pattern in 1966 ([overview in a 2016 article on question intonation](https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-01425412v1/file/Intonation%20et%20interpre%CC%81tation%20des%20questions_2016-%20vf.pdf)).
3. A question that starts with a question word falls: *Où est la pharmacie?* ("Where is the pharmacy?") The question word already says it is a question, so the voice goes down. The next lesson on questions has the words.

The rise is a strong tendency and not a fixed law. In real conversation some yes or no questions do not rise much and are understood from context. When you are unsure, rise.

### Emphasis

To show that one word matters, say it louder and longer. The OQLF calls this the **expressive accent** (*l'accent expressif*), says it is optional, and says the speaker chooses where it goes. One university source says it can fall on almost any syllable ([SFU notes](https://www.sfu.ca/fren270/Phonologie/page4_10.html)). In *C'est formidable!*, you can lean on the first syllable of *formidable* to say that something is great. Compare *C'est formidable* with a flat voice and with the lean. The meaning changes from "that is fine" to "that is wonderful".

## Hear it

Listen for the even beat inside each group and for where the voice goes up or down. The first part is a conversation with *tu*, and the second is a station announcement, which you can shadow.

```fr
Léa: Tu vas bien? | Are you well?
Samir: Oui, ça va. Et toi? | Yes, I'm fine. And you?
Léa: Super! Il y a un café ici? | Great! Is there a café here?
Samir: Oui, bien sûr. C'est formidable, non? | Yes, of course. It's great, isn't it?
Léa: Vraiment? Enfin! J'ai faim et j'ai sommeil. | Really? At last! I'm hungry and I'm sleepy.
Samir: Évidemment. C'est le matin. | Of course. It's morning.

Annonce: Bonsoir, mesdames et messieurs. Le train est à l'heure. | Good evening, ladies and gentlemen. The train is on time.
```

Some words from this lesson are short connectors that you will hear all the time. *Peut-être* /pøtɛtʁ/ means "maybe", *vraiment* /vʁɛmɑ̃/ means "really", *enfin* /ɑ̃fɛ̃/ means "at last" or "finally", *bref* /bʁɛf/ means "in short", *encore* /ɑ̃kɔʁ/ means "again" or "still", *quand même* means "all the same", *justement* means "exactly" or "as it happens", and *évidemment* means "of course". Say the next lines with the first word stressed a little.

```fr
Évidemment! | Of course!
Justement! | Exactly!
Bref, ça va. | In short, it's fine.
C'est peut-être bon. | Maybe it's good.
Merci quand même. | Thanks all the same.
Encore un café? | Another coffee?
```

## Try it

1. Count the syllables you say in *Je suis de Toronto*, then in *C'est formidable* (the last *e* is silent). Check by tapping.
2. Say *Tu es de Montréal* as a statement and then as a question. Which syllable changes?
3. Say *Où est la pharmacie?* ("Where is the pharmacy?") with a rising voice and then with a falling voice. Which one sounds right?
4. Say *C'est super* twice, once with the emphasis on *c'est* and once on *super*. Does the meaning change?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. *Je suis de Toronto* has six syllables: "je", "suis", "de", "To", "ron", "to". *C'est formidable* has four: "c'est", "for", "mi", "dable", because the final *e* is silent. Every beat must be equal.
2. The last syllable, *-al*. In the statement it falls and in the question it rises.
3. The falling voice sounds right, because the question word *où* already marks the question.
4. Yes. The emphasis points to what you care about. With the lean on *c'est*, you say that it is so, against a doubt. With the lean on *super*, you say that it is really great.

</details>

## Common mistakes

Stressing a syllable in the middle of a group, as English does. Give every syllable the same beat and lift only at the end.

Adding a vowel after a silent final *e*, so *chaise* becomes "chaise-uh". The *e* is silent and the word ends on the consonant.

Making a question-word question rise. A question that starts with *où*, *quand* or *comment* falls.

## Key terms

- **Rhythm group**: a few words said together as one unit, with the main accent on the last syllable.
- **Syllable-timed**: a language in which each syllable takes about the same time, as in French.
- **Intonation**: the rise and fall of the voice across a sentence.
- **Expressive accent**: extra force on a word you want to stress, placed where the speaker chooses.
