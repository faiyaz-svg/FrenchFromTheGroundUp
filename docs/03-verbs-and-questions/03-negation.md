# Saying no: negation

<span class="tag tag-grammar">Grammar</span> <span class="tag tag-a1">A1</span>

After this lesson you can say that something is not true, say "never", "nothing", "no one" and "no longer", and recognise how the *ne* disappears in casual speech.

## The idea

You already know two chunks with a negative inside: *je ne comprends pas* (I don't understand) from [Your first slow dialogues](../01-first-sounds-and-greetings/11-first-slow-dialogues.md), and *il n'y a pas de* from the lesson on *avoir*. A **negation** is a way of saying that something is not so. In French it has two parts that go around the verb.

### Ne ... pas

Put *ne* before the verb and *pas* after it. The verb sits between them.

| Statement | Negative |
|---|---|
| *Je parle français.* | *Je ne parle pas français.* /ʒə nə paʁl pa fʁɑ̃sɛ/ |
| *Il est de Toronto.* | *Il n'est pas de Toronto.* |
| *Nous habitons à Gatineau.* | *Nous n'habitons pas à Gatineau.* |

*Ne* is short and weak, so it follows the rules of elision. Before a vowel sound, or a silent *h*, it becomes *n'*: *je n'habite pas* /ʒə nabit pa/, *il n'est pas*. In the sound, the weight is on *pas*, which is the word that stands out.

### Pas de after un, une and des

When you say you do not have something, the article changes. *Un*, *une* and *des* become *de* (or *d'* before a vowel) after the negative.

| Statement | Negative |
|---|---|
| *J'ai une carte.* | *Je n'ai pas de carte.* |
| *Il y a un café ici.* | *Il n'y a pas de café ici.* |
| *Elle a des amis.* | *Elle n'a pas d'amis.* |

The reason is that *de* means that there is none of it, so it no longer counts "a" or "some". The articles *le*, *la* and *les* do not change: *Je n'aime pas le café.* After *être*, the article stays: *Ce n'est pas un problème.*

### Other negatives

*Pas* is the most common second part, and four more words use the same frame: *ne* before the verb and the second word after it.

| Words | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *ne ... jamais* | never | *Je ne mange jamais le matin.* |
| *ne ... rien* | nothing | *Je ne cherche rien.* |
| *ne ... personne* | no one | *Il n'y a personne ici.* |
| *ne ... plus* | no longer, not any more | *Je n'habite plus à Toronto.* |

Two positive words match the last two. *Quelque chose* means "something" and *quelqu'un* means "someone": *Tu cherches quelqu'un?* The negative answer is *Non, je ne cherche personne.* Watch out: *personne* is also the noun "a person" that you know, and in a negative sentence it means "no one".

### Short answers

You can answer a question with the second part alone, without *ne*. *Qui est là? Personne.* *Tu manges le matin? Jamais.* Two more short answers are *pas du tout*, "not at all", and *pas encore*, "not yet": *Tu as faim? Pas du tout.* *Tu as faim? Pas encore.*

When someone asks a question in the negative, *si* means "yes, in fact": *Tu n'as pas faim? Si, j'ai faim!* *Oui* would be confusing, so *si* answers a negative question.

### The spoken *ne*

In casual speech, people often leave out *ne*. Here are three examples, marked casual. *Savoir* ("to know") comes in a later stage, so learn *je sais pas* as a chunk.

```fr
Je parle pas français. {qc} | I don't speak French.
C'est pas grave. {qc} | It's no big deal.
Je sais pas. {qc} | I don't know.
```

The Vitrine linguistique of the OQLF says that dropping *ne* is common in speech and that it is discouraged in writing ([OQLF: emploi de pas seul](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/23910/la-syntaxe/la-negation-et-la-restriction/emploi-de-pas-seul)). The book teaches the full form for you to say and write. The short form is for recognising, and it is marked casual. It is common in France as well as in Quebec.

## Hear it

Two roommates in Toronto, Sara and Dan, talk in the morning. They use *tu*. Listen for the weak *ne* and the clear *pas* or *jamais*. The second part is a service desk, and the speakers use *vous*.

```fr
Sara: Tu manges ici le matin? | Do you eat here in the morning?
Dan: Non, je ne mange jamais le matin. Je n'ai pas faim. | No, I never eat in the morning. I'm not hungry.
Sara: Moi, j'ai toujours faim! Un croissant, s'il te plaît. | Me, I'm always hungry! A croissant, please.
Dan: Désolé, il n'y a plus de croissants. | Sorry, there are no more croissants.
Sara: Tu ne cherches rien? | You're not looking for anything?
Dan: Non, rien. Je n'ai pas de problème. | No, nothing. I don't have a problem.

Employée: Bonjour, vous avez une carte? | Hello, do you have a card?
Vous: Non, je n'ai pas de carte. | No, I don't have a card.
Employée: Ce n'est pas un problème. Votre nom, s'il vous plaît? | That's not a problem. Your name, please?
Vous: Je m'appelle Marc. Ça s'écrit M, A, R, C. | My name is Marc. It's spelled M, A, R, C.
```

Now the same ideas in casual speech. These lines drop *ne*, and they are marked as casual.

```fr
Dan: J'mange jamais le matin. {qc} | I never eat in the morning.
Sara: T'as pas faim? {qc} | You're not hungry?
Dan: Pas du tout. Je sais pas pourquoi. {qc} | Not at all. I don't know why.
```

## Try it

1. Make each sentence negative: *Je parle français.* *Elle habite à Toronto.* *Nous avons un chat.* *Il y a un café ici.*
2. Choose the right word, *rien*, *jamais* or *personne*: *Je ne mange ___ le matin* (never). *Il n'y a ___ ici* (no one). *Je ne cherche ___* (nothing).
3. Say the casual version of *Je ne sais pas*, the chunk for "I don't know".
4. Answer *Tu as faim?* with "not at all" and with "not yet".
5. Someone asks *Tu n'as pas de carte?* and you have one. What do you say?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. *Je ne parle pas français.* *Elle n'habite pas à Toronto.* *Nous n'avons pas de chat.* *Il n'y a pas de café ici.*
2. *Je ne mange jamais le matin.* *Il n'y a personne ici.* *Je ne cherche rien.*
3. Leave out *ne*. The casual form is the third line of the first casual list above.
4. *Pas du tout.* and *Pas encore.*
5. *Si, j'ai une carte.* *Si* answers a negative question with "yes".

</details>

## Say it

```fr
Je ne comprends pas. | I don't understand.
Je n'ai pas de carte. | I don't have a card.
Il n'y a plus de café. | There is no more coffee.
Je ne mange jamais le matin. | I never eat in the morning.
Pas du tout! | Not at all!
```

## Common mistakes

Writing *pas un café* for "no coffee". After a negative, *un*, *une* and *des* become *de*: *Je n'ai pas de café.*

Leaving out *n'* before a vowel in writing. In speech you may hear no *ne* at all, but in writing you use *je n'habite pas*.

Using *oui* to answer a negative question when you mean "yes, I do". Use *si*.

## In Quebec

Dropping *ne* is very common in casual speech in Quebec, as it is in France: *j'sais pas*, *c'est pas grave*. These forms are casual and spoken. You recognise them, and you use the full form in writing and in careful speech.

## Key terms

- **Negation**: a way of saying that something is not so, in French *ne ... pas* around the verb.
- **Pas de**: the form of *un*, *une* and *des* after a negative, meaning "none".
