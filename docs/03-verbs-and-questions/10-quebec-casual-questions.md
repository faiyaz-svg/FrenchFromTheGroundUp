# Casual questions in Quebec

<span class="tag tag-quebec">Quebec</span> <span class="tag tag-a1">A1</span>

After this lesson you can recognise the casual Quebec question ending *-tu* and a few common spoken forms (*chu*, *tsé*, *ben*, *pis*, *ouais*) when friends talk fast, and you can answer in standard French.

Before this: [Asking questions](04-asking-questions.md), [Saying no: negation](03-negation.md), [Bonjour-hi and other Montreal greetings](../01-first-sounds-and-greetings/09-bonjour-hi-montreal-greetings.md). You need: nothing.

## The idea

When Quebec friends talk to each other in a bar or at home, their speech is faster and shorter than the French in the lessons so far. This is a **register**, a way of speaking that fits a situation, and it is the casual, spoken one. It is not wrong French and it is not "bad" French. Gaétane Dostie, in an article for Usito (the Université de Sherbrooke dictionary), explains that spoken French has its own grammar, with strict rules, and that markers such as *pis* and *ben* are reduced forms with real jobs in a conversation ([Usito: les marqueurs discursifs](https://usito.usherbrooke.ca/articles/th%C3%A9matiques/dostie_1)).

In this lesson every casual form is marked `{qc}` in the examples. The book teaches standard French for you to say and write, and the casual forms for you to understand. You do not need to use them. If you do, use them only with friends.

### The -tu question

In everyday Quebec speech, a yes or no question can be made by adding a small word, *-tu*, after the verb. It carries no meaning of its own. It only says "this is a question", like the rise of the voice or *est-ce que*. Félix Tanguay writes in the journal *Correspondance* (2020) that the particle is widespread and fully part of Quebec French, and he gives examples such as *C'est-tu vrai?* ("Is it true?") and *Tu veux-tu?* ([Correspondance, 2020: le -tu interrogatif](https://correspo.ccdmd.qc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/correspondance-dou-vient-le-tu-interrogatif-et-cest-tu-pertinent-de-lenseigner.pdf)).

```fr
C'est-tu bon? {qc} | Is it good?
Tu veux-tu un café? {qc} | Do you want a coffee?
T'as-tu faim? {qc} | Are you hungry?
Elle est-tu là? {qc} | Is she there?
```

Look at *Tu veux-tu?* The first *tu* is the pronoun "you", and the second is the particle. They sound the same, so you hear *tu* twice. The particle follows the verb, and the pronoun comes before it. In Quebec, the *t* of the particle is said with a small hiss before the *u*, as in *tu* [t͡sy], which you met in [Bonjour-hi and other Montreal greetings](../01-first-sounds-and-greetings/09-bonjour-hi-montreal-greetings.md). The standard versions are the three ways from the last lesson: a rising voice, *est-ce que*, or inversion.

### Other spoken forms

| Casual (spoken) | Standard | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| *chu* | *je suis* | "I am" in speech. The University of Montreal's list of Quebec expressions gives *chu* as a contraction of *je suis* ([Université de Montréal: lexique](https://admission.umontreal.ca/fileadmin/fichiers/Conversion_2025/CON25-Lexique-expressions-quebecoises.pdf)). |
| *t'sais*, *tsé* | *tu sais* | "you know": it checks that the listener follows, or gives the speaker time (Usito article, which gives *t'sais*; *tsé* is the same word in another spelling). |
| *ben* | *bien* | a reduced *bien*, (Usito article). In *ben oui* "well, yes" it adds warmth. |
| *pis* | *puis*, *et* | "and" or "and then". *Pis?* at the start of a question means "so?" or "and then?" (Usito article). |
| *ouais* | *oui* | casual "yes". Usito marks it as familiar (*familier*), and it is also heard in France. |

*Chu* is a spoken form that you hear a lot, and in text messages some writers spell it *chu*, as you saw in [Texting to make plans](09-texting-to-make-plans.md). You will also hear *j'sais pas* for *je ne sais pas*, which follows the casual *ne* drop from [Saying no: negation](03-negation.md).

Two warnings. First, these forms are for speech. Do not use them in an email or at work. Second, how much a speaker uses them depends on the person, the age and the place. Listen first, and answer in standard French if you are unsure.

## Hear it

Two friends, Marc and Sam, are in a sports bar in Trois-Rivières. They know each other well, so they use *tu* and casual speech. Then a customer asks the same sort of question of a server in standard French, with *vous*. Listen for the *-tu* and the small words *pis*, *ben*, *tsé*.

```fr
Marc: Pis, t'as-tu faim? {qc} | So, are you hungry?
Sam: Ben oui! Chu ben content. C'est-tu bon, la poutine ici? {qc} | Well, yes! I'm really happy. Is the poutine good here?
Marc: Ouais, c'est bon, tsé. Tu veux-tu une poutine? {qc} | Yeah, it's good, you know. Do you want a poutine?
Sam: Ouais, je veux bien. {qc} | Yeah, I'd like one.
Marc: Il est huit heures moins le quart. On est à l'heure pour le match! | It's a quarter to eight. We're on time for the game!

Client: Excusez-moi, madame. Est-ce que la poutine est bonne ici? | Excuse me, ma'am. Is the poutine good here?
Serveuse: Oui, monsieur. Voulez-vous une poutine? | Yes, sir. Would you like a poutine?
Client: Oui, je veux bien. Merci beaucoup. | Yes, I'd like one. Thank you very much.
```

## Try it

1. Add *-tu* to make a casual question (the results are casual forms, for recognising): *Tu veux un café.* *C'est bon.* *T'as faim.* (*T'as* is the casual spoken form of *tu as*.)
2. These two sentences are casual spoken French. Say them in standard French: *Chu en retard.* *T'sais, chu occupé.*
3. Change the casual question *T'as-tu faim?* into two standard questions.
4. A colleague writes you an email. Should you use *-tu*? What do you use instead?
5. What does the casual *Pis toi?* mean, and what is the standard form?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. *Tu veux-tu un café?* *C'est-tu bon?* *T'as-tu faim?* (all casual)
2. *Je suis en retard.* *Tu sais, je suis occupé.*
3. *Tu as faim?* and *Est-ce que tu as faim?* (or, with *vous*, *Avez-vous faim?*).
4. No. Use standard French: *est-ce que*, or a plain question with a question mark.
5. "And you?" The standard form is *Et toi?*

</details>

## Say it

These lines are standard French, and they are the forms to say yourself.

```fr
Tu as faim? | Are you hungry?
Est-ce que c'est bon? | Is it good?
Tu veux un café? | Do you want a coffee?
Je suis en retard. | I'm late.
Et toi? | And you?
```

## Common mistakes

Taking the second *tu* of *Tu veux-tu?* for a second "you". It is a question marker.

Writing casual forms in work emails, school papers or formal letters. They are for speech and for friends.

Judging the speaker. Casual forms are a register of Quebec French, and they are not an error.

## In Quebec

This whole lesson is about Quebec speech. Tanguay's article says that the older form behind *-tu* is *-ti*, which is now used in France mostly for fun and in some regional speech. Keep to standard French when you speak and write, and use this lesson to follow the conversation around you.

## Key terms

- **Register**: how formal or casual a way of speaking is, for example casual with friends and formal at work.
- **Particle**: a small word that has no meaning of its own but marks something, such as *-tu* for a question.
