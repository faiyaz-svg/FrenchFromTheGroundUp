# Health care in Quebec for a visitor

<span class="tag tag-quebec">Quebec</span> <span class="tag tag-a2">A2</span>

After this lesson you can say which number to call or where to go in Quebec for an emergency or a small health problem, know what an Ontario health card covers in another province, and speak a few key lines at a hospital desk.

Before this: [The body, health and the pharmacy](06-body-health-and-pharmacy.md), [At the doctor and the pharmacy](07-doctor-and-pharmacy.md). You need: nothing.

This lesson gives facts as of November 2026, taken from the official pages linked below. Rules change, so check the pages before you travel. It is not medical or insurance advice.

## The idea

Imagine you live in Ontario and are visiting Montréal. You fall on the street and your foot hurts. Who do you call, and who pays? This lesson answers with what the Quebec and Ontario governments publish, and gives you the French you would use.

### Emergency: 911 and *les urgences*

The Quebec government says to go to the nearest emergency room (*les urgences*) if your life or a family member's life is in danger, and to call 911 if you cannot get there ([Quebec.ca: when to go to the emergency room](https://www.quebec.ca/en/health/health-system-and-services/service-organization/primary-care-health-and-social-services/when-go-emergency-room)). *Une ambulance* is an ambulance. On the phone you can say *J'ai besoin d'une ambulance* ("I need an ambulance").

### A small problem: 811, a clinic, a pharmacist

For a problem that is not an emergency, the same government page lists three options: Info-Santé 811 (a nurse by phone), a consultation with a health professional, and your pharmacist ([Quebec.ca: when to go to the emergency room](https://www.quebec.ca/en/health/health-system-and-services/service-organization/primary-care-health-and-social-services/when-go-emergency-room)).

Info-Santé 811 is a free and confidential phone service with a nurse. You dial 811 and choose option 1. It runs 24 hours a day, every day of the year, and the page says anglophones can be served in English. The page describes it as a service for people who live in Québec and does not say whether visitors may use it ([Quebec.ca: Info-Santé 811](https://www.quebec.ca/en/health/finding-a-resource/info-sante-811)). If you are unsure, a pharmacist or a clinic is the other first step on the list. A clinic without an appointment is called *une clinique sans rendez-vous* (literally "a clinic without an appointment"). The official pages checked for this lesson do not say how walk-in clinics treat visitors, so call the clinic first and ask.

### What an Ontario health card covers in Quebec

The Ontario government says your OHIP card is valid across Canada for some of the services it covers in Ontario: physician services and services in a public hospital that are medically necessary. It lists what is not covered: ambulance services, drugs and other medicines given outside a hospital, fees at private hospitals and facilities, and diagnostic or laboratory services outside a public hospital, among others. It recommends private health insurance for the uninsured services ([Ontario.ca: OHIP coverage across Canada](https://www.ontario.ca/page/ohip-coverage-across-canada)).

The same page says you may be charged if you do not show your valid card, or if a doctor charges you anyway. In that case you can claim a refund from OHIP, which pays at Ontario's rates, within 12 months of the service. The page does not mention Quebec by name, so these are the general rules for other provinces.

So there are three money words to know. *Une assurance* is insurance. *Des frais* are fees, the costs you pay. *Un remboursement* is a refund. You might say *Je voudrais un remboursement* only after you have paid and have a receipt, and the claim goes to OHIP, not to the clinic.

The Quebec health insurance board is the RAMQ (*Régie de l'assurance maladie du Québec*), and you will see its name on forms and signs ([RAMQ](https://www.ramq.gouv.qc.ca/en)). Your Ontario card is not a RAMQ card.

## Hear it

An Ontario visitor, Dev, hurt his foot in Montréal. He goes to the emergency room desk and speaks with a receptionist (*vous*). Listen for *tombé*, *depuis* and the words for the card.

```fr
Réceptionniste: Bonjour, monsieur. Qu'est-ce qui ne va pas? | Hello, sir. What's wrong?
Dev: Bonjour. Je suis tombé et j'ai très mal au pied. | Hello. I fell and my foot really hurts.
Réceptionniste: Depuis quand? | Since when?
Dev: Depuis une heure. | For an hour.
Réceptionniste: Vous avez une carte d'assurance maladie? | Do you have a health insurance card?
Dev: J'ai ma carte de l'Ontario. Voici. J'ai aussi une assurance privée. | I have my Ontario card. Here it is. I also have private insurance.
Réceptionniste: Merci. Asseyez-vous, s'il vous plaît. Un infirmier va vous voir. | Thank you. Please sit down. A nurse will see you.
Dev: Merci. Je ne sais pas combien ça coûte. Est-ce que je dois payer ici? | Thanks. I don't know how much it costs. Do I have to pay here?
Réceptionniste: Je ne sais pas. Demandez à l'infirmier. | I don't know. Ask the nurse.
```

*Je ne sais pas* is the honest answer when you do not know the rule, and it is useful for you too. *Demandez* is the *vous* command of *demander*.

## Try it

1. Which number do you call if your life is in danger and you cannot get to the emergency room?
2. Name two things an Ontario health card does not cover outside Ontario, according to the Ontario page.
3. Match: *une assurance*, *des frais*, *un remboursement*. (a) fees (b) insurance (c) refund
4. Say in French: "I fell and my back hurts." and "I have an Ontario card."
5. Who would you ask first if you are unsure whether a clinic takes visitors?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. 911.
2. Any two of: ambulance services, medicines given outside a hospital, fees at private hospitals, laboratory services outside a public hospital.
3. *une assurance* = (b), *des frais* = (a), *un remboursement* = (c).
4. *Je suis tombé(e) et j'ai mal au dos.* *J'ai une carte de l'Ontario.*
5. The clinic itself, by phone, or a pharmacist.

</details>

## Say it

```fr
Je suis tombé et j'ai mal au pied. | I fell and my foot hurts.
J'ai besoin d'une ambulance. | I need an ambulance.
J'ai une carte de l'Ontario. | I have an Ontario card.
Est-ce que je dois payer ici? | Do I have to pay here?
Je ne sais pas. | I don't know.
```

## Common mistakes

Saying *Je vais à urgences*. The word is plural and takes *aux*: *Je vais aux urgences.*

Forgetting the agreement when a woman speaks: *Je suis tombé*. She writes *Je suis tombée*, because *tomber* uses *être*.

Saying *J'ai besoin un médecin*. After *avoir besoin* you need *de*: *J'ai besoin d'un médecin.*

## In Quebec

*Les urgences* is plural: *Je vais aux urgences.* A clinic is *une clinique*, and *sans rendez-vous* means "without an appointment". The 811 page says service is available in English. At a desk or a clinic you cannot count on it, so the lines in Say it are worth learning.
