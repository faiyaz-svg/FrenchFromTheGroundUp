# The body, health and the pharmacy

<span class="tag tag-words">Words</span> <span class="tag tag-a2">A2</span>

After this lesson you can name the main parts of the body, say where it hurts and how you feel, and ask for a medicine at a pharmacy counter.

Before this: [Avoir and il y a](../02-sounds-spelling-and-everyday-things/07-avoir-and-il-y-a.md), [Reflexive verbs](../06-habits-memories-and-food/04-reflexive-verbs.md), [Prepositions of place](../04-people-places-and-directions/05-prepositions-of-place.md). You need: nothing.

This lesson gives you words, not medical advice. If you are ill or hurt, ask a doctor or a pharmacist. The next lessons show how to find help in Quebec.

## The idea

Health talk uses a small set of body words, a few fixed phrases with *avoir mal*, and the verb *se sentir* ("to feel"). Learn the nouns with their article, as always, because the article tells you the gender.

### Parts of the body

| Part | Words |
|---|---|
| Head | *la tête*, *le visage* (face), *le nez*, *la bouche*, *l'oreille* (f.), *les yeux* (eyes), *une dent* (tooth), *la gorge* (throat) |
| Upper body | *le cou* (neck), *l'épaule* (f.), *le bras*, *la main*, *le doigt*, *le dos* (back), *le ventre* (belly), *l'estomac* (m.) |
| Legs | *la jambe*, *le genou* (knee), *le pied* |
| Whole body | *le corps*, *la peau* (skin), *le cœur* |

*Les yeux* is the irregular plural of *l'œil*, which you met in stage 2. *Le cœur* is the heart. *L'estomac* is the stomach and *le ventre* is the belly, the part of the body outside.

### Saying where it hurts

To say that a part of your body hurts, use *avoir mal à* + the part: *J'ai mal à la tête.* ("My head hurts.") You know that *à* joins with *le* and *les*, so you get four forms.

| Body part | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| masculine | *au* | *J'ai mal au dos. J'ai mal au pied.* |
| feminine | *à la* | *J'ai mal à la gorge. J'ai mal à la jambe.* |
| vowel | *à l'* | *J'ai mal à l'estomac. J'ai mal à l'oreille.* |
| plural | *aux* | *J'ai mal aux dents. J'ai mal aux mains.* |

French uses *le*, *la* or *les* with the body part, not "my". You say *J'ai mal à la tête*, not *J'ai mal à ma tête*. Two other phrases are worth learning whole: *un mal de tête* ("a headache") and *Ça fait mal.* ("It hurts."). A question you will hear is *Où est-ce que vous avez mal?* ("Where does it hurt?")

### Symptoms and illnesses

A **symptom** is a sign that someone is ill. *Je tousse* means "I cough", and *la toux* is the cough. *Un rhume* is a cold, *la grippe* is the flu and *la fièvre* is a fever. *Une douleur* is a pain. The word *malade* means "ill", and it does not change between masculine and feminine.

With *la fièvre*, *avoir* uses the partitive, as for food: *J'ai de la fièvre.* For a cold or the flu, use *avoir* with the noun: *J'ai un rhume. J'ai la grippe.*

### How you feel

*Se sentir* ("to feel") is reflexive, like *se lever*. It works like *partir*: *je me sens, tu te sens, il se sent, nous nous sentons, vous vous sentez, ils se sentent.* After it you put an adjective or an adverb: *Je me sens fatigué. Elle se sent malade. Je me sens mal. Je me sens mieux.* *Mal* ("badly") and *mieux* ("better") are the adverbs from stage 6.

### At the pharmacy

*Une pharmacie* is a pharmacy and *un pharmacien* or *une pharmacienne* is the pharmacist. A **medicine** is *un médicament*, and a tablet is *un comprimé*. The paper from a doctor that lets you get a medicine is *une ordonnance*. The pharmacist usually starts with *Je peux vous aider?* and you answer with what is wrong: *J'ai mal à la gorge. Vous avez quelque chose pour la toux?*

## Hear it

Mme Roy is a pharmacist in Gatineau. A customer comes to the counter. They use *vous*. This is a language example, not medical advice. Listen for the body words after *mal à*.

```fr
Pharmacienne: Bonjour, monsieur. Je peux vous aider? | Hello, sir. Can I help you?
Client: Bonjour. J'ai mal à la gorge et je tousse. | Hello. I have a sore throat and I'm coughing.
Pharmacienne: Vous avez de la fièvre? | Do you have a fever?
Client: Non, mais je me sens très fatigué. | No, but I feel very tired.
Pharmacienne: Vous avez une ordonnance? | Do you have a prescription?
Client: Non, je n'ai pas d'ordonnance. | No, I don't have a prescription.
Pharmacienne: Voici un médicament pour la toux. Ça coûte douze dollars. | Here is a medicine for the cough. It costs twelve dollars.
Client: Merci. Je prends un comprimé? | Thanks. Do I take one tablet?
Pharmacienne: Oui. Je vais vous expliquer comment le prendre. | Yes. I'll explain to you how to take it.
```

Listen to *les dents* /le dɑ̃/ and *le doigt* /lə dwa/: the last letters are silent.

## Try it

1. Say where it hurts: *la tête*, *le dos*, *l'estomac*, *les dents*.
2. Say in French: "I feel tired." "I have a headache." "I have a cold."
3. Name the body part: it is at the end of the arm, with five fingers; it is in the middle of the leg; you hear with it.
4. Ask the pharmacist for something for the cough, and say you don't have a prescription.
5. Say in French: "My feet hurt." and "I feel better."

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. *J'ai mal à la tête. J'ai mal au dos. J'ai mal à l'estomac. J'ai mal aux dents.*
2. *Je me sens fatigué.* *J'ai mal à la tête.* (or *J'ai un mal de tête.*) *J'ai un rhume.*
3. *la main, le genou, l'oreille.*
4. *Vous avez quelque chose pour la toux? Je n'ai pas d'ordonnance.*
5. *J'ai mal aux pieds.* *Je me sens mieux.*

</details>

## Say it

```fr
J'ai mal à la tête. | I have a headache.
J'ai mal aux dents. | My teeth hurt.
Je me sens malade. | I feel ill.
Où est-ce que vous avez mal? | Where does it hurt?
Je n'ai pas d'ordonnance. | I don't have a prescription.
```

## Common mistakes

Saying *J'ai mal à ma tête*, a word-for-word copy of English. Use the article: *J'ai mal à la tête*.

Writing *à* and *le* apart before a masculine body part. They join: *J'ai mal au dos*.

Using *je sens* alone for "I feel ill": *Je me sens mal*. The reflexive *me* is needed when you talk about yourself.

## In Quebec

The English word "prescription" covers two things in French. The OQLF's Grand dictionnaire terminologique says that the English word "prescription" covers both the doctor's order and the paper it is written on, and that the written form must be called *une ordonnance* ([OQLF: ordonnance](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/fiche-gdt/fiche/1198768/ordonnance)). So at a pharmacy, ask *Vous avez une ordonnance?* and say *J'ai une ordonnance*.
