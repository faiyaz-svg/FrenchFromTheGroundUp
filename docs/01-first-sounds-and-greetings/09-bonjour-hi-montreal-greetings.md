# Bonjour-hi and other Montreal greetings

<span class="tag tag-quebec">Quebec</span> <span class="tag tag-a1">A1</span>

After this lesson you can answer the mixed greeting "Bonjour, hi" in French, reply to *merci* with standard phrases, and understand *bienvenue* and *bonne fin de semaine* in Quebec.

Before this: [Greetings and politeness](08-greetings-and-politeness.md). You need: nothing.

## The idea

Montreal is a French-speaking city where English is also everywhere. In shops, cafés and restaurants downtown, many workers greet a customer with both languages at once: "Bonjour, hi." The greeting lets the customer choose. This lesson shows what it means, how to answer it, and a few Quebec habits around thanks and goodbyes. These are customs and not rules, so treat them as things you will hear.

### Bonjour-hi

"Bonjour, hi" is a greeting that mixes *bonjour* and English "hi". The Canadian Press reported in April 2024 that French-only greetings were dropping in Montreal and "bonjour, hi" was gaining popularity ([CTV News](https://www.ctvnews.ca/montreal/article/french-only-greetings-drop-in-montreal-as-bonjour-hi-gains-popularity)). People in Quebec hold different views on it, and this book does not take a side. It only says that you will hear it, and what you can do when you do.

Nothing is wrong with your French. Often the greeting offers both languages, and you choose. If you want to practise, answer in French, with a plain *bonjour*. Then continue in French. If the worker switches to English, you can keep going in French. You may also say the sentence again more slowly. It is your decision, and neither choice is rude.

### Answering merci

You learned *de rien* for "you're welcome". Two other standard phrases are *je vous en prie*, which is more formal, and *il n'y a pas de quoi*, which means "it's nothing".

In Quebec you will also hear *bienvenue* alone as an answer to *merci*. The OQLF says that this use is a **calque** (a phrase copied word for word) of English "you're welcome", and it advises against it. It recommends *je vous en prie*, *de rien* or *il n'y a pas de quoi*, and says *bienvenue* should be a greeting for welcoming someone ([OQLF: emploi déconseillé de l'emprunt bienvenue](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/21469/les-emprunts-a-langlais/emprunts-semantiques/emploi-deconseille-de-lemprunt-bienvenue)). So the book teaches the standard answers for you to say, and *bienvenue* for you to recognise.

The word *bienvenue* is fully correct when you welcome someone, as in *Bienvenue à Montréal!* ("Welcome to Montreal!").

### Bonne fin de semaine

*Bonne fin de semaine!* means "Have a good weekend." It uses *la fin de semaine* for "the weekend". The OQLF recommends *fin de semaine* in Quebec and Canada. It says the English borrowing *week-end* is accepted in European French, and that *week-end* is also fairly common in Quebec, where the OQLF does not recommend it ([OQLF: fin de semaine](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/redirection/ficheuid/8361442)). In France, *bon week-end* is the usual greeting. Both mean the same.

## Hear it

Listen for the first words of the worker, and for how the customer answers in French. The customer uses *vous*, as people do with a worker they do not know. The last line shows the casual *bienvenue* reply, which is marked as Quebec.

```fr
Boulangère: Bonjour, hi! | Hello, hi!
Vous: Bonjour. Un pain blanc, s'il vous plaît. | Hello. A white loaf, please.
Boulangère: Voilà. | Here you are.
Vous: Merci beaucoup. | Thank you very much.
Boulangère: Bienvenue. Bonne fin de semaine! {qc} | You're welcome. Have a good weekend!
Vous: Vous aussi, au revoir. | You too, goodbye.
```

### The standard answers

Here is the same exchange with a standard reply.

```fr
Vous: Merci beaucoup. | Thank you very much.
Boulangère: De rien. Bonne journée! | You're welcome. Have a good day!
Vous: Au revoir. | Goodbye.
```

## Try it

1. A worker says "Bonjour, hi!" Write one answer in French.
2. Someone says *merci*. Give two standard answers.
3. Someone answers your *merci* with *bienvenue*. What does it mean, and what do you say back?
4. Translate "Have a good weekend" in the Quebec way.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. *Bonjour.* You can add a request: *Bonjour. Un café, s'il vous plaît.*
2. *De rien*, *je vous en prie* or *il n'y a pas de quoi*.
3. It means "you're welcome". You do not need to answer. You can say *merci* again or *bonne journée*.
4. *Bonne fin de semaine.*

## Say it

```fr
Bonjour. Un café, s'il vous plaît. | Hello. A coffee, please.
Merci beaucoup. | Thank you very much.
De rien. Bonne journée! | You're welcome. Have a good day!
Je vous en prie. | You're welcome (formal).
Bonne fin de semaine! | Have a good weekend!
```

## Common mistakes

Taking "Bonjour, hi" as a sign that your French is not good enough. It is a common shop greeting that offers a choice.

Using *bienvenue* to answer *merci* when you speak. Use *de rien*, *je vous en prie* or *il n'y a pas de quoi* instead, and recognise *bienvenue*.

Saying *je vous en prie* to a friend. It is formal, so use *de rien* with friends.

## In Quebec

This whole lesson is about Quebec habits. *Bienvenue* as a reply to *merci* is a Quebec habit that the OQLF advises against, and *bonne fin de semaine* is the form the OQLF recommends in Quebec for the weekend greeting. The Montreal greeting is a mix of French and English, and you can answer with *bonjour* and continue in French.
