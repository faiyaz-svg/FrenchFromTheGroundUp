# Your first slow dialogues

<span class="tag tag-listen">Listen</span> <span class="tag tag-a1">A1</span>

After this lesson you can follow three short conversations in French by ear, find names, places and greetings in them, and use a routine for listening to any dialogue in this book.

Before this: [Être and introducing yourself](10-etre-and-introductions.md). You need: headphones if you have them.

## The idea

Listening is a skill, and it grows by method. Reading a French line and understanding it is one thing. Hearing the same line at speed, with no text, is another. The dialogues below use only what stage 1 has taught, so almost every word is one you have met, and the few new ones are glossed and listed at the end. Your task is to hear them, not to learn new words.

### How to listen

On the site, each French line has a play button, and the English translation stays hidden behind a "Show translation" button. Keep it hidden. Use this routine for each dialogue.

1. Set the voice to slow speed and Quebec, in the page header.
2. Press play all and listen once. Do not read the French or the English.
3. Play it again and listen for the three things the questions ask: names, places and greetings.
4. Play each line, stop, and repeat it out loud. Match the lift at the end of the line.
5. Answer the questions without looking back. Then open the translation to check.
6. Switch to normal speed and play it a last time. Notice which lines you can now follow.

If you miss a line twice, read the French and then play it again. That is a normal step, not a failure. What matters is the second listen without the text.

## Hear it

### Dialogue 1: at a hotel desk

The guest and the receptionist do not know each other, so they use *vous*. Listen for how the name is spelled, and for where the guest is from.

```fr
Réceptionniste: Bonsoir, monsieur. | Good evening, sir.
Vous: Bonsoir. Je m'appelle Paul Tremblay. | Good evening. My name is Paul Tremblay.
Réceptionniste: Ça s'écrit comment? | How is that spelled?
Vous: T, R, E, M, B, L, A, Y. | T, R, E, M, B, L, A, Y.
Réceptionniste: Merci. Vous êtes de Montréal? | Thank you. Are you from Montreal?
Vous: Non, je suis de Québec. | No, I'm from Quebec City.
Réceptionniste: Voilà. Merci beaucoup. | Here you are. Thank you very much.
```

### Dialogue 2: two students

Inès and Jacob are students of the same age, so they use *tu*. Listen for the two cities.

```fr
Inès: Salut! Je m'appelle Inès. Et toi? | Hi! My name is Inès. And you?
Jacob: Salut, je m'appelle Jacob. | Hi, my name is Jacob.
Inès: Enchantée. Tu es de Montréal? | Nice to meet you. Are you from Montreal?
Jacob: Oui, je suis de Montréal. Et toi? | Yes, I'm from Montreal. And you?
Inès: Moi, je suis de Gatineau. | I'm from Gatineau.
Jacob: Enchanté, Inès. | Nice to meet you, Inès.
```

### Dialogue 3: a phone call between friends

Amélie answers a personal line with *allô*, as in [Greetings and politeness](08-greetings-and-politeness.md). Listen for how she answers the phone and how the call ends.

```fr
Amélie: Allô? | Hello?
Karim: Allô, Amélie. C'est Karim. Ça va? | Hello, Amélie. It's Karim. How's it going?
Amélie: Très bien, merci. Et toi? | Very well, thanks. And you?
Karim: Ça va bien. Bonne fin de semaine! | Good. Have a good weekend!
Amélie: Merci, toi aussi. Au revoir! | Thanks, you too. Goodbye!
```

## Try it

1. Dialogue 1: How is the surname spelled, and where is the guest from?
2. Dialogue 1: Do the speakers use *tu* or *vous*? Which word tells you?
3. Dialogue 2: Where is Inès from, and where is Jacob from?
4. Dialogue 3: What word does Amélie say to answer the phone, and what does Karim wish her at the end?
5. Which dialogue was easiest to follow, and which line did you need to replay most?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. T, R, E, M, B, L, A, Y (Tremblay). The guest is from Quebec City.
2. *Vous*. The words *vous êtes* in the receptionist's question tell you.
3. Inès is from Gatineau. Jacob is from Montreal.
4. *Allô*. Karim wishes her *bonne fin de semaine*, a good weekend.
5. Your own answer. Replay your hardest line three times and then say it aloud.

</details>

## Say it

Pick the line you replayed most in each dialogue and say it aloud.

```fr
Je m'appelle Paul Tremblay. | My name is Paul Tremblay.
Je suis de Québec. | I'm from Quebec City.
Moi, je suis de Gatineau. | I'm from Gatineau.
Allô, c'est Karim. Ça va? | Hello, it's Karim. How's it going?
Bonne fin de semaine! | Have a good weekend!
```

## Common mistakes

Reading the translation first. Keep it hidden, listen twice, and check at the end.

Trying to catch every word. Listen for names, places and greetings, and let the rest pass.

Giving up after one listen. A first listen is for the shape of the conversation, and the second and third are for the details.

## In Quebec

You will hear *allô* when people answer a personal phone call. The sound of the voice in the audio is the Quebec setting if you chose it. Try the France setting once and notice how the same lines differ.
