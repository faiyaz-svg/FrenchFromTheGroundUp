# How French spelling, sounds and rhythm work

<span class="tag tag-sounds">Sounds</span> <span class="tag tag-a1">A1</span>

After this lesson you can read a short French word aloud with the right stress, explain why many letters are silent, and name what each accent does.

Before this: [Set up voices and audio](../00-start-here/set-up-voices-and-audio.md). You need: headphones if you have them.

## The idea

French spelling looks hard because it keeps letters that people stopped saying long ago. The reading direction is more regular than the writing direction: once you know what a letter group says, it says the same thing almost every time. This stage teaches the sounds one family at a time. This first lesson gives you the map.

### Many final letters are silent

In *petit* /pəti/ (small) the final *t* is silent. In *grand* /gʁɑ̃/ (big, tall) the *d* is silent, and in *trop* /tʁo/ (too much) the *p* is silent. In *français* /fʁɑ̃sɛ/ (French) the final *s* is silent too. A first rule: the last consonant of a word is often silent. The common exceptions are *c*, *r*, *f* and *l* (the letters of the English word "careful"). You hear the *r* in *bonjour* /bɔ̃ʒuʁ/.

The rule has more exceptions, and [Spelling rules and liaison](../02-sounds-spelling-and-everyday-things/03-spelling-sound-rules-and-liaison.md) covers them. For now, when you meet a new word, look at the end and ask whether the last letter could be silent.

### Accents are part of the spelling

An **accent** is a small mark above (or below) a letter. It is part of the spelling: *thé* /te/ (tea) is spelled with the mark, and leaving it out is a spelling error. Accents do not move the stress. French has five of them.

| Mark | French name | Letters | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ´ | *accent aigu* | é | Says the closed vowel /e/ | *café* /kafe/ |
| ` | *accent grave* | è, à, ù | On *è*, says the open vowel /ɛ/. On *à* and *ù*, the sound does not change, and the mark tells two words apart | *père* /pɛʁ/, *où* /u/ |
| ^ | *accent circonflexe* | ê, â, î, ô, û | On *ê*, says /ɛ/. Often marks a letter that was dropped long ago | *fête* /fɛt/, *hôtel* /otɛl/ |
| ¨ | *tréma* | ë, ï | Says this vowel separately from the one before it | *Noël* /nɔɛl/ |
| ¸ | *cédille* | ç | Makes *c* say /s/ before *a*, *o* or *u* | *garçon* /gaʁsɔ̃/ |

Plain *c* before *a*, *o* or *u* says /k/, as in *café*, so the cedilla in *garçon* is a real change in sound. *Où* (where) sounds the same as *ou* (or), and only the grave accent separates them on paper.

## Hear it

French has no strongly stressed syllable inside a word. Every syllable gets about the same weight, and the voice rises a little on the last syllable of a group of words. English does the opposite: *CAN-a-da* leans on the first syllable. Listen for the even beat in these lines and for the small lift at the end of each one.

```fr
Bonjour. | Hello.
Merci. | Thank you.
Le café, le thé. | The coffee, the tea.
Le garçon est petit. | The boy is small.
```

Play each line twice. Then say it with the same weight on every syllable and lift only the last one. A common mistake is to stress the first syllable, which makes *bonjour* sound like English "BON-jur".

### Sounds on paper: IPA

Spelling shows sounds only roughly, so this book also uses the **International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)**. It is a set of symbols where each symbol always means one sound. IPA goes between slashes: *bonjour* /bɔ̃ʒuʁ/. You learn a few symbols in each lesson and never need the whole chart. Three appear already. The symbol /ʃ/ is the *sh* of "shoe", /ʒ/ is the *s* of "measure", and /ʁ/ is the French *r*, made in the throat. [French consonants](../02-sounds-spelling-and-everyday-things/01-consonants.md) teaches it. The vowel symbols come in lessons 3 to 6.

### Quebec and France

Quebec French and the French of France share one spelling and one grammar. The sounds differ a little, mostly in some vowels and in how *t* and *d* sound before *i* and *u*. Each vowel lesson in this stage has a short Quebec note, written in square brackets: [t͡sy] is how *tu* often sounds in Montreal. For now, say the standard sound and learn to recognise the Quebec one when you hear it.

## Try it

1. Which final letters are silent in *petit*, *trop* and *grand*? Check each against the audio.
2. Name the accent on each: *café*, *père*, *fête*, *Noël*, *garçon*.
3. Say *bonjour* and *merci* with even weight on every syllable and a lift on the last. Record yourself and listen back.
4. In *garçon*, what would the *c* say without the cedilla?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. The final *t* in *petit*, the final *p* in *trop*, and the final *d* in *grand*.
2. *Café*: accent aigu. *Père*: accent grave. *Fête*: accent circonflexe. *Noël*: tréma. *Garçon*: cédille.
3. Your check: no syllable is louder than the rest, and the voice rises only at the end.
4. /k/, as in *café*. The cedilla makes it /s/.

</details>

## Common mistakes

Stressing the first syllable, as English does. French gives every syllable about the same weight and lifts on the last one.

Reading every letter. In *petit* the final *t* is silent, so you say /pəti/.

Treating an accent as optional. *Thé* without its accent is a spelling error, and the drills mark it "almost".

## In Quebec

Quebec speakers use the same spelling and the same stress pattern. The voice setting lets you hear a Quebec voice and a France voice and compare them. The sound differences are small and are taught one by one in the next lessons.

## Key terms

- **Accent**: a small mark on a letter that changes its sound or tells two words apart.
- **IPA**: the International Phonetic Alphabet, a set of symbols where each symbol is one sound.
- **Silent letter**: a letter that is written but not said, such as the final *t* in *petit*.
