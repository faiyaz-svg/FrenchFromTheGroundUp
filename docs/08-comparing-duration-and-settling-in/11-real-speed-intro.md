## The idea

Slow French and real French are not far apart, but they are not the same. When people talk fast, they drop small sounds, join words and shorten common phrases. You already know a few Quebec forms from [Casual questions in Quebec](../03-verbs-and-questions/10-quebec-casual-questions.md): *chu* for *je suis* and *tsé* for *tu sais*. This lesson adds the reductions you hear in everyday speech, in Montréal and elsewhere. The spellings below show the sound. They are not forms you write in a formal text, and you say the standard form yourself while you learn.

### How speech gets shorter

Three things happen. First, an *e* that is not stressed disappears, as you saw with elision in [Spelling rules and liaison](../02-sounds-spelling-and-everyday-things/03-spelling-sound-rules-and-liaison.md). *Je* loses its vowel, and the consonants join. Second, whole short words fall away: *il* disappears from *il faut* and *il y a*. Third, two words become one sound. The table shows the common ones.

| You hear | Standard | Sound | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| *chais pas* | *je ne sais pas* | /ʃɛ pa/ | I don't know |
| *j'sais pas* | *je ne sais pas* | /ʒsɛ pa/ | I don't know |
| *y'a* | *il y a* | /ja/ | there is, there are |
| *t'as* | *tu as* | /ta/ | you have |
| *t'es* | *tu es* | /tɛ/ | you are |
| *faut* | *il faut* | /fo/ | you have to, one must |
| *p't'être* | *peut-être* | /ptɛtʁ/ | maybe |
| *chu* | *je suis* | /ʃy/ | I am |
| *tsé* | *tu sais* | /tse/ | you know |

In *chais pas*, the two sounds /ʒ/ and /s/ of *je sais* merge into one /ʃ/, because /ʒ/ loses its voice next to the /s/. The *ne* of *je ne sais pas* has already gone, as in [Casual questions in Quebec](../03-verbs-and-questions/10-quebec-casual-questions.md). *Chu* is a Quebec form, and the University of Montréal's list of Quebec expressions gives *chu* for *je suis* ([Université de Montréal: lexique](https://admission.umontreal.ca/fileadmin/fichiers/Conversion_2025/CON25-Lexique-expressions-quebecoises.pdf)). The others (*y'a*, *t'as*, *t'es*, *faut*, *p't'être*) are heard in casual speech in Quebec and elsewhere, and they stay in speech.

### Filler words

A **filler** is a small word that gives the speaker time to think or links two ideas. *Euh* is the sound of hesitating, like "um". *En fait* means "in fact" or "actually". *Du coup* means "so" or "as a result": Usito lists it as familiar and gives *en conséquence* as its meaning ([Usito: coup](https://usito.usherbrooke.ca/définitions/coup)), so use it in casual speech. *Franchement* means "frankly". *Carrément* means "totally" or "completely". *Tu vois* is "you see". Learn to hear them and let them pass, because they carry little meaning. In writing, drop them.

### Four steps for listening

1. Play the fast version once without stopping. Catch two or three words, for example a place, a number or a name.
2. Play the slow version, and read the French with it.
3. Play the fast version again. Now you expect the reduced forms, so you hear *y'a* as *il y a* and *t'es* as *tu es*.
4. Say the fast version yourself, line by line. Shadowing is the best way to train your ear.

## Hear it

Camille and Yanis are friends in their thirties and use *tu*. They talk fast in a Montréal café. Each clip has a fast version, marked casual, and then a slow one. The fast lines are casual speech, so they carry the casual flag even where the form is also heard outside Quebec.

Clip 1, about the café.

```fr
Camille: Chais pas, y'a trop de monde ici. {qc} | I don't know, there are too many people here.
Camille: Je ne sais pas, il y a trop de monde ici. | I don't know, there are too many people here.
Yanis: T'as faim, toi? T'es fatigué? {qc} | Are you hungry? Are you tired?
Yanis: Tu as faim, toi? Tu es fatigué? | Are you hungry? Are you tired?
```

Clip 2, about plans.

```fr
Camille: Faut partir à cinq heures. {qc} | We have to leave at five.
Camille: Il faut partir à cinq heures. | We have to leave at five.
Yanis: P't'être qu'on prend un café avant? {qc} | Maybe we'll have a coffee first?
Yanis: Peut-être qu'on prend un café avant? | Maybe we'll have a coffee first?
```

Clip 3, with fillers.

```fr
Yanis: Euh, en fait, j'ai pas vraiment faim. Du coup, je prends juste un thé. {qc} | Um, actually, I'm not really hungry. So I'll just have a tea.
Yanis: Euh, en fait, je n'ai pas vraiment faim. Alors, je prends seulement un thé. | Um, actually, I'm not really hungry. So I'll just have a tea.
```

Clip 4, with Quebec forms.

```fr
Camille: Chu ben contente, tsé. Mon frère arrive demain. {qc} | I'm really happy, you know. My brother arrives tomorrow.
Camille: Je suis très contente, tu sais. Mon frère arrive demain. | I'm very happy, you know. My brother arrives tomorrow.
Yanis: Franchement, c'est carrément mieux ici, tu vois. {qc} | Honestly, it's totally better here, you see.
Yanis: Franchement, c'est vraiment mieux ici, tu vois. | Honestly, it's really better here, you see.
```

## Try it

1. Write the standard form of each: *chais pas*, *y'a*, *t'as*, *t'es*, *faut*, *p't'être*.
2. Say each reduced form out loud with its standard form, three times in a row at speed.
3. Play the fast line of clip 3 without reading. Write down the three filler words you hear. Then check the text.
4. Which filler means "so, as a result": *en fait*, *du coup* or *franchement*? Which means "actually"?
5. Say the fast line of clip 4 as a shadow, then say the slow line. Notice how much shorter the first one is.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. *je ne sais pas*, *il y a*, *tu as*, *tu es*, *il faut*, *peut-être*.
2. Check that *chais pas* is two quick syllables, /ʃɛ pa/.
3. *euh*, *en fait*, *du coup*.
4. *Du coup* means "so". *En fait* means "actually".
5. The fast line drops *je suis* to *chu* and *très* to *ben*.

## Common mistakes

Writing reduced forms in a formal text or an email to a boss: *t'as*, *faut*, *chais pas*. Write the standard forms.

Trying to translate every word in a fast clip. Catch the key words first and replay.

Treating fillers as part of the message. *Euh* and *en fait* carry little meaning, so let them pass.

## In Quebec

*Chu* and *tsé* come with *ben* and *pis* (you met these in [Casual questions in Quebec](../03-verbs-and-questions/10-quebec-casual-questions.md)). Usito marks *du coup* as familiar. Listen for these forms without copying them at first. Your own French stays standard, and your ear learns the casual forms.

## Key terms

- **Filler**: a small word such as *euh* or *en fait* that fills a pause or links ideas, with little meaning of its own.
- **Reduction**: a shorter spoken form of a word or phrase, such as *chais pas* for *je ne sais pas*.
