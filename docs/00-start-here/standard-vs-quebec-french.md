# Standard and Quebec French

This page explains what the book teaches, and what is different in Quebec French. Quebec French is a full variety of French with its own accent, words and habits. It is not slang and it is not bad French.

## What the book teaches

The book teaches standard French for speaking and writing. That is the French of schools, official documents, news and tests, and it is understood everywhere French is spoken, including Quebec. It also teaches you to understand Quebec French from the first stage, because in Montreal you will hear it all day.

When the book shows a Quebec form, it says so. A Quebec word has the label "Quebec", and a casual spoken form is marked `{qc}` on the page and described as casual. You learn the standard form first and the casual form to recognise.

## What differs

Quebec and France share one grammar and one spelling. The differences are mostly in sounds, some words, casual forms and habits of address.

## Tu and vous

French has two words for "you". *Vous* is polite and also the plural. *Tu* is for friends, family and children. The basics are in [Greetings and politeness](../01-first-sounds-and-greetings/08-greetings-and-politeness.md).

Habits differ by region and by person. In Quebec, a shop worker or a younger colleague may say *tu* to you quickly, and you may hear *tu* in places where *vous* would be normal in France. When you are not sure, use *vous* and wait to see what the other person uses. A polite *vous* rarely causes offence.

## Greetings and sacres

*Bonjour* works for hello. In Montreal you will also hear *bonjour-hi*, a shop greeting that mixes both languages. It is taught in [Bonjour-hi and other Montreal greetings](../01-first-sounds-and-greetings/09-bonjour-hi-montreal-greetings.md). Quebec also has strong swear words called *sacres*. They are taught as words to recognise and not to use, in Sacres: recognise, don't use<!-- link: ../12-real-montreal/05-sacres-recognise-dont-use.md -->.

## Why standard French comes first

- Tests and government work expect standard written French. A bilingual public service job tests it.
- Every French speaker understands it, in Montreal, in Ottawa and in Paris.
- Casual Quebec forms are built on the standard ones. Once you know *je suis*, you can see how *chu* comes from it.
- Quebec speakers use standard French too, in writing, in formal speech and on the radio.

## Register matters in every variety

People in every French-speaking place shift between formal and casual. A Montrealer writing to a client uses the same standard forms as a Parisian, and a Parisian with friends uses casual forms too. The labels in this book (formal, neutral, casual, Quebec, France, spoken, written) mark where a form belongs.
