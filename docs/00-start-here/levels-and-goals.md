# Levels and goals

This page explains the CEFR levels, the levels used by the federal public service and the Ontario Public Service, and how the book's four lines compare. The comparison is approximate, and the page says why.

## The CEFR levels in plain words

The Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR) has six levels. They describe what you can do in a language, not what you know about it.

| Level | Name | What you can do |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | Beginner | Understand and use everyday phrases. Introduce yourself, ask and answer simple questions about where you live and what you need, if the other person speaks slowly |
| A2 | Elementary | Handle simple, routine exchanges: shopping, directions, work basics. Describe your background and your surroundings in short sentences. Write a short message |
| B1 | Intermediate | Cope with most situations when travelling. Talk about experiences, plans and opinions in connected sentences. Follow the main points of clear speech on familiar topics. Write simple connected text |
| B2 | Upper intermediate | Follow the main ideas of complex text and fast speech on concrete and abstract topics. Talk with native speakers without strain on either side. Write clear, detailed texts and explain a point of view |
| C1 | Advanced | Understand long, demanding texts and implied meaning. Express yourself fluently and almost without searching for words. Use the language flexibly for work and study |
| C2 | Proficiency | Understand almost everything with ease. Express yourself precisely, including fine differences in meaning, even in complex situations |

The book goes from A1 to B2. It does not cover C1 or C2.

## Federal levels for reading, writing and oral

The federal public service uses its own scale of three levels, A, B and C, for each of three skills: reading, writing and oral interaction. The [Treasury Board qualification standards in relation to official languages](https://www.canada.ca/en/treasury-board-secretariat/services/staffing/qualification-standards/relation-official-languages.html) describe them. In plain words, as read there in 2026:

| Level | Reading | Writing | Oral |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Simple texts on familiar work topics, such as routine forms and short emails | Isolated words and simple phrases, with errors allowed if the message is clear | Simple questions and instructions on familiar topics, with many errors |
| B | The overall meaning of most work texts, and specific details | Short, factual texts on concrete work topics | Conversations on concrete topics, descriptions and factual explanations. Hypothetical and sensitive topics are not expected |
| C | Abstract or unfamiliar topics and subtle meaning, such as policies and contracts | Detailed, well-structured texts on complex topics, such as briefing notes | Clear, detailed explanations on complex, abstract or sensitive topics, opinions and hypothetical situations |

A position's required profile is written as three letters in the order reading, writing, oral. A profile of BBB means B in all three. A profile of CBC means C in reading, B in writing and C in oral. A bilingual position may ask for any of these.

Since June 20, 2025, new appointments to bilingual supervisory positions that supervise employees in bilingual regions need at least CBC. Before that, the minimum was BBB. See the [Directive on Official Languages for People Management](https://www.tbs-sct.canada.ca/pol/doc-eng.aspx?id=26168) for the rule and its exceptions. Current office holders are not covered by the change until the position is filled again. Check the directive and the job ad for the exact profile of any role you want.

## Ontario government jobs

Bilingual job ads in the Ontario Public Service ask for oral and written French at an "advanced" or "advanced-plus" level, and candidates are tested before they are hired. This page says no more than that, because the test format is not described in what was found. Search current ads on [gojobs.gov.on.ca](https://www.gojobs.gov.on.ca/) and read the language requirement in each one.

## How the book's lines compare

No official table says that a CEFR level equals a federal level. The sources used for this book do not give one. The table below is a rough guide to help you plan, not a promise of a test result. Tests judge what you do under test conditions, and your own skills vary: many learners read better than they speak.

| Line | CEFR | Approximate federal level | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Green | A1 | Below A | Everyday survival French |
| Orange | A2 | Around A | Routine exchanges and short messages |
| Yellow | B1 | Working toward B | Concrete work conversations and short factual texts, with errors |
| Blue | B2 | Around B, working toward C | Reading and speaking on more abstract topics. Stage 15 and 16 practise the formal writing and spoken argument that C asks for |

Reaching C in the federal system usually takes more than the book's B2 end point. Treat the end of stage 16 as a strong base, then add reading of policy documents, writing briefing notes and speaking practice with a coach or teacher.

## Setting your goal

Your target is undecided, so you do not have to pick one now. The first 8 stages serve every goal. After stage 8, look at your goal:

- Conversations and travel in Quebec: stages 9 to 12 are the core. Spend extra time on the Quebec lessons and the listening lists.
- Texts and emails: the writing lessons in stages 9 to 16.
- A bilingual job: stages 13 to 16, and then a test-prep course for the specific test your employer uses.
