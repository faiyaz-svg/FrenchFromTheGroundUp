# Table culture in Quebec

<span class="tag tag-quebec">Quebec</span> <span class="tag tag-a2">A2</span>

After this lesson you can use the Quebec names of the three meals (*déjeuner*, *dîner*, *souper*), name some Quebec dishes and places to eat, understand *un cinq à sept*, and talk about the tip (*le pourboire*) without a wrong guess.

## The idea

Quebec shares most of its vocabulary with France, but a few everyday food words differ, and some customs differ too. This lesson names each difference and gives the source. Everything here is a custom or a common use. It is not a rule that every person follows.

### The three meals

In Quebec, the meal names move back one meal compared with France. The OQLF (the Quebec language office) lists *déjeuner* as the usual word for breakfast in Quebec ([OQLF: déjeuner](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/redirection/ficheuid/8368956)), and says *petit-déjeuner* is used mainly in formal language and in material for tourists. It lists *dîner* as the word for the midday meal in Quebec, and notes that in France *déjeuner* is the usual word for it ([OQLF: dîner](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/redirection/ficheuid/26511882)). For the evening meal it lists *souper* for Quebec and Canada, and *dîner* for France ([OQLF: souper](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/redirection/ficheuid/8872574)). In Canada, *dîner* for the evening meal is mainly used in formal and protocol settings. The same page notes that *souper* is still usual in several regions of France.

| Meal | Quebec | France |
|---|---|---|
| morning | *le déjeuner* | *le petit-déjeuner* |
| midday | *le dîner* | *le déjeuner* |
| evening | *le souper* | *le dîner* |

Watch out: if a friend from France invites you to *déjeuner*, they mean lunch. If a friend from Quebec invites you to *déjeuner*, they mean breakfast. Ask the time if you are unsure: *À quelle heure?*

### Dishes you will meet

*La poutine* you know from stage 2. Here are others.

*Le pâté chinois* is a baked dish with three layers: seasoned cooked ground meat at the bottom, then corn, then mashed potatoes on top. The OQLF says the earliest written uses in Quebec date from the 1930s, and that the origin of the name is unknown ([OQLF: pâté chinois](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/fiche-gdt/fiche/8873150/pate-chinois)).

*La tourtière* is a pie filled with ground pork and spices. The OQLF says *tourtière* is the usual word for this pie in most of Quebec, except in the regions of Charlevoix, Saguenay and Lac-Saint-Jean and their edges, where the same word names a different dish ([OQLF: pâté à la viande](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/redirection/ficheuid/2091350)). In those regions, you should ask what the dish is.

*Un sandwich au smoked meat* is a sandwich of smoked and cured beef. The OQLF records the English words as the accepted term in Quebec, and says the dish came to Montreal with Eastern European immigrants ([OQLF: sandwich au smoked meat](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/redirection/ficheuid/8359098)). *Une tarte* is a pie, and *un bagel* is a bagel. Both you can order by name.

### Where people eat

*Un casse-croûte* is a small restaurant that makes simple dishes fast, with a counter where customers sit on stools. The OQLF recommends this word over *snack-bar* ([OQLF: casse-croûte](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/redirection/ficheuid/2298340)). Simple foods such as *une poutine* and *une crème molle* (soft ice cream) are common there. The OQLF's own term is *crème glacée molle* ([OQLF: crème glacée molle](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/fiche-gdt/fiche/2091996/creme-glacee-molle)).

### Un cinq à sept

*Un cinq à sept* is a gathering of colleagues or friends for drinks in the late afternoon, roughly 5 to 7 p.m. It often takes place after work, in a bar. The OQLF says to write it out in full: *un cinq à sept*, and not *un 5 à 7* ([OQLF: cinq à sept](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/24798/lorthographe/cas-particuliers-lies-a-lorthographe/orthographe-et-graphie-de-cinq-a-sept)). The OQLF accepts *cinq-à-sept* with hyphens too. If you are invited, ask *C'est à quelle heure?*

### La cabane à sucre

*Une cabane à sucre* is a sugar shack: a place where maple sap is boiled into syrup (*le sirop d'érable*, from *un érable*, a maple tree). The season runs from late February or early March to the end of April or early May, and meals include pea soup, ham, eggs, tourtière and baked beans, with maple desserts ([Tourisme Montréal: sugar shack 101](https://mtl.org/en/experience/sugar-shack-101), 2026). The famous dessert is *la tire d'érable sur la neige*: hot maple syrup on snow. Many shacks only open for a few weeks, so book ahead.

### Le pourboire

*Un pourboire* is a tip. In France, the law says menu prices include service and that a tip is optional ([DGCCRF](https://www.economie.gouv.fr/dgccrf/les-fiches-pratiques/pourboire)). In Quebec, a tip left freely is different from a service charge written on the bill ([Revenu Québec: pourboires et frais de service](https://www.revenuquebec.ca/fr/entreprises/taxes/tpstvh-et-tvq/situations-particulieres-liees-a-la-tpstvh-et-a-la-tvq/alimentation-application-de-la-tps-et-de-la-tvq/pourboires-et-frais-de-service/)). Customs differ from person to person and place to place, and payment terminals offer suggested amounts, so this book gives no percentage. A good habit is to look at the amounts the terminal suggests, or to ask a friend.

## Hear it

Listen for the meal names in each line. A family in Saguenay has lunch (*le dîner*), and they use *tu*. Then two friends talk about a sugar shack outing in March.

```fr
Céline: À table! Le dîner, c'est à midi. | To the table! Lunch is at noon.
Lucas: Qu'est-ce qu'on mange, grand-mère? | What are we eating, grandmother?
Céline: Du pâté chinois et de la salade. Et de la tarte au sucre pour le dessert. | Pâté chinois and salad. And sugar pie for dessert.
Lucas: Super! Et pour le souper? | Great! And for supper?
Céline: Ce soir, nous mangeons de la soupe. | Tonight we are eating soup.

Maude: C'est le mois de mars, c'est le temps de la cabane à sucre! | It's March, it's sugar shack time!
Dev: Qu'est-ce qu'on mange? | What do we eat?
Maude: De la soupe aux pois, du jambon, des œufs, de la tourtière. | Pea soup, ham, eggs, tourtière.
Dev: Et la tire d'érable sur la neige? | And the maple taffy on snow?
Maude: Oui, c'est du sirop d'érable chaud sur de la neige. C'est délicieux! | Yes, it's hot maple syrup on snow. It's delicious!
```

## Try it

1. What does a person from Quebec call the midday meal? The evening meal? Breakfast?
2. A friend from France invites you to *déjeuner*. What meal do they mean?
3. Write *un 5 à 7* the way the OQLF recommends.
4. Which dish has meat, corn and mashed potato in layers?
5. Say in French: "Tonight we're eating soup" and "Lunch is at noon."

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. *le dîner*, *le souper*, *le déjeuner*.
2. Lunch, the midday meal.
3. *un cinq à sept*.
4. *le pâté chinois*.
5. *Ce soir, nous mangeons de la soupe.* *Le dîner, c'est à midi.*

</details>

## Say it

```fr
Le déjeuner, le dîner, le souper. | Breakfast, lunch, supper.
Le souper, c'est à 18 h. | Supper is at 6 p.m.
Je voudrais une poutine, s'il vous plaît. | I'd like a poutine, please.
On va à la cabane à sucre en mars. | We're going to the sugar shack in March.
Un cinq à sept, c'est à quelle heure? | A cinq à sept, what time is it?
```

## Common mistakes

Using *dîner* for the evening meal in Quebec. Use *souper* for supper, and keep *dîner* for lunch.

Writing *un 5 à 7*. The OQLF says to write *un cinq à sept*.

Giving a tip percentage as a fixed rule. Customs differ, so look at what the terminal suggests or ask.
