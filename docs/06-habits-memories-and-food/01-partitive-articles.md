# Partitive articles: du, de la, des

<span class="tag tag-grammar">Grammar</span> <span class="tag tag-a2">A2</span>

After this lesson you can say "some bread", "some soup" and "some water" with *du*, *de la*, *de l'* and *des*, say "no coffee" and "a lot of patience" with *de*, and choose between *le café* and *du café*.

## The idea

So far you know two kinds of article. The **definite article** (*l'article défini*: *le*, *la*, *l'*, *les*) points to a known or general thing. The **indefinite article** (*l'article indéfini*: *un*, *une*, *des*) points to one or several things you can count: *un croissant*, *des œufs*.

Some things you cannot count one by one: water, bread, patience, soup. For these French has a third kind. The **partitive article** (*l'article partitif*) means "some, a part of": *du pain*, *de la soupe*, *de l'eau*. English often leaves "some" out ("I want soup"), but French cannot. A noun in French nearly always has an article.

### The four forms

| Noun | Partitive | Example |
|---|---|---|
| masculine, consonant | *du* | *du pain*, *du café*, *du lait* |
| feminine, consonant | *de la* | *de la soupe*, *de la salade* |
| before a vowel sound (m. or f.) | *de l'* | *de l'eau*, *de l'argent* |
| plural | *des* | *des œufs* |

You already met *du* in [Prepositions of place](../04-people-places-and-directions/05-prepositions-of-place.md) (*près du parc*). There it was *de* + *le*, "of the". Here *du* is a different word that happens to look the same, and the meaning ("some") tells them apart. *Des* works the same way: it is the plural of *un*, and it is also the plural "some".

### Partitive or indefinite?

Use *un* or *une* when you count a unit: *un café* is one cup. Use *du* for the substance in general: *Je bois du café le matin* means "I drink coffee in the morning". Both are right in different situations. *Un café, s'il vous plaît* is an order of one cup. *Tu veux du café?* offers "some coffee".

### Definite or partitive?

This is the choice that causes most mistakes. With verbs of liking (*aimer*, *adorer*, *détester*) French uses the definite article, because you like the thing in general: *J'aime le café*, *Elle adore la soupe*. With verbs of having, eating, drinking and buying, you use a part: *Je bois du café*, *Il mange de la soupe*.

| You say | Article | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *J'aime le café.* | *le* | I like coffee (in general) |
| *Je bois du café.* | *du* | I drink (some) coffee |

### After a negative: de

In a negative sentence with *ne ... pas*, *du*, *de la*, *de l'*, *des*, *un* and *une* all become *de* (*d'* before a vowel): *Je bois du café* becomes *Je ne bois pas de café*. You met this with *il n'y a pas de* and *je n'ai pas de carte*. The same happens after *ne ... plus* and *ne ... jamais*: *On n'a plus de lait*.

Watch out: *être* is the exception. After *ce n'est pas* the article stays: *Ce n'est pas du café* ("It is not coffee").

### After a quantity: de

After a word of quantity, French uses *de* alone: *beaucoup de pain*, *un peu de soupe*, *assez d'eau* ("enough water"). The quantity words come in [Food, shopping and quantities](03-food-shopping-and-quantities.md). For now remember *beaucoup de* ("a lot of") and *un peu de* ("a little").

### Some set phrases

Several fixed phrases use the partitive: *avoir de la chance* ("to be lucky"), *avoir de la patience*, *faire du sport*. Weather, noise and crowds use it too: *Il y a du soleil*, *Il y a du bruit*, *Il y a beaucoup de monde*. And *avoir de l'argent* is "to have money". In stage 3 you learned *faire du sport* as a chunk. Now you can read it: *faire* + *du* + *sport* is "to do some sport". The same pattern gives *faire de la cuisine* and *faire du ski*.

One more rule: in careful writing, *des* becomes *de* when an adjective stands before a plural noun: *de bons gâteaux*, *de beaux jours*.

## Hear it

Listen for the vowel in *du* /dy/, the same /y/ as in *tu*, and for the short *de* in *de la*, *de l'* and *pas de*. Two roommates in a Toronto kitchen use *tu*.

```fr
Nadia: Tu as faim? | Are you hungry?
Sam: Oui, un peu. Il y a du pain? | Yes, a bit. Is there any bread?
Nadia: Oui, et il y a de la soupe. Tu veux de la soupe? | Yes, and there is soup. Do you want some soup?
Sam: Je veux bien. Et de l'eau, s'il te plaît. | Yes, please. And some water, please.
Nadia: Il n'y a pas de lait, mais il y a des œufs. | There's no milk, but there are eggs.
Sam: On n'a plus de café! | We have no coffee left!
Nadia: Oh non. Je vais faire les courses ce soir. | Oh no. I'm going to do the grocery shopping tonight.
```

## Try it

1. Add the partitive article: *___ eau*, *___ café*, *___ soupe*, *___ œufs*, *___ pain*.
2. Make each sentence negative: *Il y a du lait.* *Je bois du café.* *On a de l'eau.*
3. *le* or the partitive? *J'aime ___ thé.* *Je bois ___ thé le matin.* *Tu veux ___ soupe?*
4. Say in French: "He is lucky." "I have a lot of patience."
5. Write *faire du ski* in the negative: "I don't ski" (use *faire*).

<summary>Answer</summary>

1. *de l'eau*, *du café*, *de la soupe*, *des œufs*, *du pain*.
2. *Il n'y a pas de lait.* *Je ne bois pas de café.* *On n'a pas d'eau.*
3. *J'aime le thé.* *Je bois du thé le matin.* *Tu veux de la soupe?*
4. *Il a de la chance.* *J'ai beaucoup de patience.*
5. *Je ne fais pas de ski.* The article becomes *de* after the negative.

## Say it

```fr
Je bois du café le matin. | I drink coffee in the morning.
Tu veux de la soupe? | Do you want some soup?
Il n'y a pas de lait. | There is no milk.
J'aime le café, mais je ne bois pas de café le soir. | I like coffee, but I don't drink coffee in the evening.
Elle a de la chance. | She is lucky.
```

## Common mistakes

Leaving out the article: *Je bois café*. French needs *du café*, even when English says "I drink coffee".

Keeping *du* after a negative: *Je ne bois pas du café*. After *ne ... pas* use *de*: *Je ne bois pas de café*.

Using *du* with a verb of liking: *J'aime du café*. Use the definite article: *J'aime le café*.

## Key terms

- **Partitive article**: *du*, *de la*, *de l'* or *des*, meaning "some", used for a part of something you do not count.
- **Definite article**: *le*, *la*, *l'* or *les*, used for a known or general thing.
- **Indefinite article**: *un*, *une* or *des*, used for one or several countable things.
